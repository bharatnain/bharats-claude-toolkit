# Talking to Users — customer discovery that produces truth

**Why this matters to you:** Every startup decision you'll make — what to build, what to charge, whether to quit your job — rests on beliefs about what other people want. Almost everyone gets this evidence-gathering step wrong. People you interview will lie to you politely, and your own brain will hear what it wants to hear. The founders you've watched succeed weren't smarter about products. They were more disciplined about extracting truth from conversations before writing code. This doc gives you the complete toolkit: how to ask questions that produce real answers, how to grade those answers by strength, and a week-by-week plan to validate one of your candidate ideas in 4-6 weeks. Master this and you'll have something most first-time founders never get: conviction backed by evidence instead of hope.

---

## 1. The core problem: everyone lies to you (nicely)

**[Evergreen]** Steve Blank is the Stanford professor whose book *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* founded the "customer development" school — the idea that you develop your understanding of customers with the same rigor you develop your product. His work directly inspired the Lean Startup movement, the now-standard approach of testing startup ideas with small, cheap experiments. Blank compressed the whole discipline into one line: **"No facts exist inside the building, only opinions."** ([The Four Steps to the Epiphany summary](https://www.businessfloss.com/books/the-four-steps-to-the-epiphany)). His method has four steps — customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation, company building — and the first two happen *before* you scale anything. The traditional approach (build the product, then find customers) is precisely backwards. Startups fail not for lack of product but for lack of customers.

But "get out of the building" isn't enough, because once you're out there, people lie. Not maliciously — socially. If you tell someone your idea and ask what they think, they will encourage you, the way your mom would. That's the premise of Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*, the single most useful book on this topic. Construct your questions so that **even your mother couldn't lie to you** — meaning they ask about facts, not feelings about your idea ([The Mom Test, Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/10670144-the-mom-test-1-talk-about-their-life-instead-of)).

The lies come in three flavors:
- **Compliments** ("That's a cool idea!") — worthless; they cost the speaker nothing.
- **Fluff** ("I would totally use that") — hypothetical future behavior, which humans are terrible at predicting.
- **Ideas** ("You should add X!") — a distraction; users are good at articulating problems, bad at designing solutions.

Everything below is machinery for filtering these out.

## 2. The Mom Test, in depth

**[Evergreen]** Fitzpatrick's three rules ([The Mom Test — mtlynch.io book report](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/10670144-the-mom-test-1-talk-about-their-life-instead-of)):

1. **Talk about their life instead of your idea.** The moment you mention your solution, you've contaminated the conversation. They stop describing their problems and start reacting (politely) to your pitch. Ideally the person doesn't even know you have an idea.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.** "Do you ever X?" and "Would you Y?" invite fiction. "When did you last X? Walk me through it" retrieves memory, which is much harder to fake.
3. **Talk less and listen more.** If you're talking more than ~30% of the time, you're pitching, not learning.

### Good vs. bad questions

| Bad question (fails the Mom Test) | Why it fails | Good replacement |
|---|---|---|
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Invites a compliment | "How do you deal with [problem] today?" |
| "Would you buy a product that did X?" | Hypothetical future; people over-promise | "What have you already tried or paid for to solve this?" |
| "How much would you pay for this?" | Fantasy pricing; costs them nothing to inflate | "What does this problem cost you now — in dollars, hours, or missed deals?" |
| "Do you ever have trouble with [problem]?" | Leading; everyone says yes to be agreeable | "What's the hardest part of [doing the broader task]?" |
| "Would your team use this?" | Speculation about other people | "Who else on your team feels this pain? Can you introduce me?" |
| "Do you think it's a problem that X?" | Puts your framing in their mouth | "Tell me about the last time X happened. What did you do next?" |

**[Evergreen]** Eric Migicovsky founded Pebble (the smartwatch company) and later became a partner at Y Combinator — YC, the world's best-known startup accelerator, a program that funds and coaches very early startups. In YC's free Startup School lecture ["How to Talk to Users"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MT4Ig2uqjTc), he teaches five questions that work for almost any problem-discovery interview ([reference guide](https://medium.com/@davegoldblatt/startup-school-reference-guide-for-lecture-3-how-to-talk-to-users-with-eric-migicovsky-f4afd41b8e9d)):

1. "What is the hardest part about [doing the thing]?"
2. "When is the last time you encountered this problem?"
3. "Why was that hard?" (the answer here is your future marketing copy — people buy resolutions to *their* framing of the pain)
4. "What, if anything, have you done to try to solve it?"
5. "What don't you love about the solutions you've tried?"

Question 4 is the killer. **If someone hasn't tried to solve a problem — not even a spreadsheet, a Google search, a duct-tape workaround — the problem is probably not painful enough to build a company on.** Existing workarounds are the strongest evidence of a real problem, and their flaws are your product spec.

### Deflect compliments

**[Evergreen]** Fitzpatrick calls compliments "shiny, distracting, and worthless" ([mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)). When you get one — "wow, I love this" — do not write it in your notes as a positive signal. Deflect and redirect: "Thanks — but I'm more interested in how you handle this today. When did it last come up?" Track a mental (or literal) tally. Compliments logged as data are how founders talk themselves into building things nobody wants.

### Dig for commitment and advancement

**[Evergreen]** A meeting that ends with "sounds great, keep me posted!" is a *failed* meeting — you learned nothing and got nothing. Fitzpatrick's rule: every serious conversation should end by asking for a **commitment** (they give up something of value) or an **advancement** (the relationship moves concretely toward a sale). The currencies of commitment, in ascending order of strength ([mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)):

- **Time** — a scheduled follow-up with an agenda, a working session, hours spent trialing your prototype.
- **Reputation** — an introduction to their boss, their team, or peers ("who else should I talk to?" is both a research question and a commitment test).
- **Money** — a letter of intent, a pre-order, a deposit, a signed pilot.

If they won't give up any of these, their enthusiasm was politeness. More on this hierarchy in §4.

## 3. How many interviews, and how to find people before you have a product

### How many

**[Evergreen]** The principle: interview until answers become predictable — until you can finish interviewees' sentences. **[2026]** Current benchmarks come from Lenny's Newsletter (a widely read newsletter about product and startups, written by former Airbnb product lead Lenny Rachitsky) and its research into how top startups actually validated:

- **B2B** (business-to-business — selling to companies): the median successful founder talked to **~30 potential customers before committing**; many did far more. Zip's founders did 75 interviews in 2-3 weeks. Ramp's team talked to 100+ finance teams. Gusto's co-founder committed to 30 conversations before writing any code. Amplitude's founder says 30 was too few and he should have done 50 ([How to validate your B2B startup idea — Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-validate-your-b2b-startup)).
- **Consumer** (selling to individuals): roughly **20+ interviews** to see patterns. The source here is Todd Jackson — a partner at First Round Capital, a well-known venture capital firm that invests at the earliest stage; before that he was a product leader on Gmail and at Facebook. He cites Good Dog's ~20 dog-owner interviews. Note that consumer validation leans more on behavioral tests — see §6 ([How to validate your startup idea — Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea)).
- Fitzpatrick's floor: after 3-5 conversations you'll have signal on obvious dead-ends. If answers are still all over the place after 10, your **customer segment is too broad** — a segment is the specific group of people you're targeting. Slice it narrower: not "small businesses" but "independent restaurant owners with 2-5 locations" ([mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)).

**[Evergreen]** Teresa Torres wrote *Continuous Discovery Habits*, the standard text on making customer conversations a permanent practice rather than a phase. Her gold standard: **weekly touchpoints with customers, forever**, done by the people actually building the product ([Continuous Discovery Habits summary](https://andrewclark.co.uk/product-book-summaries/continuous-discovery-habits)). Discovery isn't a gate you pass once; it's a habit. But at your pre-idea stage, think of it as a concentrated sprint (see the playbook in §10).

### How to find interviewees with no product and no audience

**[Evergreen]** In rough order of effort:

1. **Second-degree network.** Not your friends (see anti-patterns, §8) — your friends' colleagues. Ask for intros with a forwardable blurb: "I'm researching how [role] handles [task] — not selling anything, just 20 minutes of questions."
2. **Where your users already congregate online.** Subreddits, Slack/Discord communities, LinkedIn groups, niche forums. Don't post "would you use my app?" — post "I'm researching how people handle X, can I buy 30 minutes of your time?" Reading existing complaint threads is free discovery before you talk to anyone.
3. **Cold outreach** — contacting strangers who don't know you. For B2B, send targeted LinkedIn messages or emails to people with the exact job title you're studying. Response rates are low (5-15% is normal for well-targeted, personalized notes); it's a numbers game. Offering a $50-100 gift card for 30 minutes is standard and worth it. **[2026]** AI tools make personalizing 100 outreach messages an afternoon's work — but everyone knows this now, so genuinely specific first lines ("saw your post about X") outperform generic AI polish.
4. **Go where they physically are.** Migicovsky's advice: **"Drop by in person!"** — industry meetups, conferences, trade shows, or literally the coffee shop where your target users work ([lecture guide](https://medium.com/@davegoldblatt/startup-school-reference-guide-for-lecture-3-how-to-talk-to-users-with-eric-migicovsky-f4afd41b8e9d)). Founders underuse this because it's uncomfortable. That's exactly why it works.
5. **End every interview with "who else should I talk to?"** Two referrals per interview compounds: 5 seed interviews → 10 → 20.

Being in NYC helps you: it's dense with every industry vertical (finance, media, healthcare, retail, legal). For nearly any B2B idea, your first 30 interviewees are within a 30-minute subway ride.

## 4. The signal-strength hierarchy: opinions < time < reputation < money

**[Evergreen]** This is the most important mental model in this document. Grade every piece of "validation" by what it cost the person giving it:

```
WEAKEST                                                    STRONGEST
opinions ──────< time ──────< reputation ──────< money
"cool idea"    trials it,     intros you to     pays, pre-orders,
"I'd use it"   books the      their boss/team   signs an LOI or pilot
               follow-up
```

What each money-tier instrument proves, and how to ask:

- **LOI (letter of intent)** — a non-binding written statement that a company intends to buy your product when it exists, ideally naming a price and timeframe. Proves: the economic buyer (the person who controls the budget — see §5) will put their name on paper. That's reputation plus a whiff of money. Doesn't prove: they'll actually pay. LOIs are legally toothless, and buyers know it. How to ask: "If we build exactly what we discussed at $X/month, would you sign a one-page letter saying you intend to be one of our first customers? It helps us prioritize who we build for." A specific price and name on the LOI makes it 10x more meaningful than a vague one.
- **Pre-order / deposit** — actual money before the product exists. Proves: real willingness to pay at a real price. Even a $20 deposit filters fantasy from intent better than fifty enthusiastic interviews. How to ask: "We're taking $99 pre-orders for the first batch — fully refundable. Want in?"
- **Design partner agreement** — a company agrees to meet with you weekly, share their workflow and data, and shape the product, usually in exchange for free or discounted early access. Proves: sustained time commitment plus reputation (they're exposing internal processes to you). This is the standard B2B instrument at the prototype stage ([Lenny's B2B validation](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-validate-your-b2b-startup)). How to ask: "We're selecting 3-5 design partners who'll get the product shaped around their workflow and locked-in early pricing. It costs you a 30-minute call every two weeks and honest feedback." Aim for 3-5. One design partner makes you a consultancy building their custom tool.
- **Paid pilot** — a fixed-scope, fixed-term paid engagement with defined success criteria. $1k-$25k for a 1-3 month pilot is a common early-stage range; the amount matters less than the fact of payment. Proves: budget exists, the buying process works, and they'll champion it internally. This is the strongest pre-product signal available. How to ask: "Rather than a free trial, we do paid pilots — $5k for 8 weeks, scoped to [outcome], and it converts to an annual contract if we hit [metric]. Free pilots don't get internal attention; paid ones do." (That last sentence is true, and buyers know it.)

**[Evergreen]** Todd Jackson's four "signs your idea has legs" map cleanly onto this hierarchy: (1) people pay you — ideally strangers, not friends; (2) they keep *using* an ugly prototype despite its flaws; (3) strong emotion — "the moment you asked about their current provider, they started cursing, literally" (a real founder quote from Lenny's research); (4) cold inbound — people you never contacted find you and ask for access ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-validate-your-b2b-startup)). Notice none of the four is "people said they liked it."

## 5. Interview scripts: problem discovery vs. solution validation

These are different interviews with different rules. Run them in this order, never mixed in one conversation's first half.

### Script A — Problem discovery (no idea mentioned; 25-30 min)

Goal: map how they actually work and where it hurts. You may not mention your idea until the final minutes, if at all.

1. *Context (5 min):* "Walk me through your role. What does a typical Tuesday look like?" — you need their world, not your slice of it.
2. *Task walk-through (10 min):* "Tell me about the last time you [did the task area]. Start from the beginning — what triggered it, what tools, what happened next?" Probe every friction point with "why was that hard?" and "what happened then?"
3. *Migicovsky's five (10 min):* hardest part → last occurrence → why hard → what have you tried → what's wrong with those attempts.
4. *Cost quantification:* "Roughly how much time/money does this eat per week/month? Who else does it affect?"
5. *Close (2 min):* "This was really useful. Two asks: who are two other people I should talk to? And can I follow up when I have something to show you?" (Both are commitment tests.)

### Script B — Solution validation (prototype or mockup in hand; 30 min)

Goal: test whether your specific solution earns commitment. Still mostly Mom-Test rules — but now you *do* show the thing, late, and ask for something real.

1. *Re-verify the problem (5 min):* re-run the "last time this happened" question. If the pain doesn't reappear unprompted, note it — you may have a solution hunting a problem.
2. *Demo — then shut up (10 min):* show the prototype and let them react. Silence is your friend. Watch for them grabbing the mouse, asking "can it do X for *my* case," or trying to use it on real data — behavior beats commentary.
3. *Workflow fit (5 min):* "Where exactly would this slot into what you showed me last time? What would you stop doing?" If they can't name what it replaces, adoption will stall.
4. *The money conversation (5 min):* not "would you pay?" (see the trap below) but: "What are you paying today for [current tool/workaround, including labor]?" then "We're planning to charge $X. Reaction?" Watch faces, not words. Too-fast agreement means you've priced too low or they're being polite.
5. *The ask (5 min):* pick one from the hierarchy — follow-up working session, intro to the economic buyer, LOI, pilot. **End every solution interview with a concrete ask.** The response *is* the data.

### B2B specifics: buyer vs. user, and the "would you pay" trap

**[Evergreen]** In B2B, three roles usually belong to different people: the **user** (who feels the pain), the **economic buyer** (who controls the budget), and the **champion** (who advocates for you internally). An analyst can love your tool and be irrelevant to whether it sells. A VP can buy tools her team refuses to adopt. Discovery is incomplete until you've interviewed both sides: users for the workflow, buyers for budget, priority, and the buying process. Ask the buyer: "Who approved the last tool you bought? What did that process look like? How long did it take?" — past-tense questions, so they pass the Mom Test. Migicovsky's prototype-stage advice is to get **numerical** answers: what does this problem cost, how often does it occur, who has budget authority ([lecture guide](https://medium.com/@davegoldblatt/startup-school-reference-guide-for-lecture-3-how-to-talk-to-users-with-eric-migicovsky-f4afd41b8e9d)).

The **"would you pay?" trap**: any yes to a hypothetical payment question is worth approximately nothing — the word "would" does all the lying. Replace it with (a) what they currently pay for alternatives (a fact), (b) a specific price attached to a specific ask ("$500/month pilot starting March — yes or no?"), or (c) an actual instrument from §4. A "yes, but not now / talk to procurement / circle back next quarter" is a **no** wearing a suit.

## 6. Consumer discovery specifics: tests, not interviews

**[Evergreen]** Consumer behavior is even less predictable from interviews than B2B — people cannot forecast their own habits. So consumer discovery leans on **behavioral tests**, where people vote with clicks, emails, and dollars:

- **Landing page test (a.k.a. smoke test):** a one-page website describing the product as if it exists, with a call-to-action — the one thing you ask visitors to do, such as an email signup, "join waitlist," or a fake "Buy — $29" button. Drive paid traffic to it and measure conversion (the share of visitors who take the action). Also called **fake door tests** or "pretotyping" — testing the *pretense* of a product before building it ([Fake Door Testing guide — GetHorizon](https://www.gethorizon.net/guides/fake-door-testing)).
- **Fake door test:** the same idea applied to a feature — a button or menu item for something that doesn't exist yet; clicking it reveals "coming soon — want early access?" It measures demand at near-zero cost. Ethical footnote: reveal the truth immediately and give clickers something (an early access list), or you burn trust.
- **Waitlist / two-page test:** Buffer's canonical example. Before writing any product code, founder Joel Gascoigne put up a two-page site. Page one described the idea (schedule tweets without flooding your followers); page two collected emails. He tweeted the link. When interest looked real, he inserted a *pricing page* between the two, so a visitor had to click a specific plan before reaching the signup form: one extra click that tested both willingness to pay and which price point drew clicks. Gascoigne never published how big that pre-launch email list was — his post describes the signal qualitatively (enough signups, and enough clicks on paid plans, to convince him) — but it was clearly modest by "viral waitlist" standards: he built the product in evenings and weekends over ~7 weeks, and got his **first paying customer within 4 days of launch**, with ~4% of the first ~500 users upgrading to paid ([Joel Gascoigne, "Idea to Paying Customers in 7 Weeks" — Buffer blog](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/)). The lesson isn't the waitlist size; it's the escalating-commitment design. (If you've heard the *giant* pre-launch waitlist story, that's Dropbox — their explainer video reportedly took the beta list from ~5,000 to ~75,000 overnight; different company, different test.) A waitlist where people *refer friends to move up the list* upgrades the signal from time to reputation.

### Worked example: a $500 landing page test

Say one of your candidate ideas is a consumer app for splitting group-travel expenses. **[2026]** Rough current numbers (verify against your own campaign — ad prices move):

- Budget: **$500** on Instagram/TikTok ads targeting a tight audience (e.g., US 24-35, travel-interest).
- At ~$1.50-2.50 CPC (cost per click — what you pay per ad click), $500 buys roughly **200-330 visits**. Call it 250.
- **Measure two conversions:** visits → email signup, and (stronger) signup → "reserve your spot for $5" click.
- Reading the results: for a landing page shown to cold traffic (strangers who arrived from an ad, not fans), **~1-3% email conversion is meh, 5-10% is interesting, 15%+ is a strong signal**. But treat these as rough heuristics, not pass/fail gates. A low score often means weak copy or the wrong audience rather than a dead idea ([fake door testing in 2026 — Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/fake-door-testing/)). So: 250 visits × 8% = 20 emails = promising; 250 × 1% = 2-3 emails = rework the pitch or audience before concluding anything.
- The killer follow-up: email those 20 people asking for a 15-minute call. If 5+ take the call, you have humans spending *time* — one rung up the hierarchy — and your first interview pipeline, for $500 and a weekend.

Run 2-3 headline/audience variants; the variant that wins tells you the message *and* the segment.

## 7. Concierge, Wizard-of-Oz, and prototype MVPs: do things that don't scale

**[Evergreen]** First, the acronym: **MVP (minimum viable product)** — the smallest thing you can put in front of users that tests your riskiest assumption. Three pre-product flavors:

- **Concierge MVP:** you deliver the service *manually and visibly* — you're the product. Want to test an AI meal-planning app? Personally plan meals for 10 customers over WhatsApp for $20/month. You learn the real workflow and whether people pay, before any code. Vanta and Ramp both started on this "do-it-manually" path ([Lenny's B2B validation](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-validate-your-b2b-startup)).
- **Wizard-of-Oz MVP:** the interface looks automated, but humans are behind the curtain doing the work. It tests whether people want the *product experience* at the intended price, while you learn what to automate. (Concierge = they know it's manual; Wizard-of-Oz = they don't.)
- **Prototype + design partners:** a clickable mockup built in Figma (a browser-based design tool for making realistic fake screens) or **[2026]** an AI-generated working demo. Current AI coding tools can produce a credible demo in days, which has made prototype-stage validation dramatically cheaper than it was even in 2023. The bar for "impressive demo" has risen accordingly. What still can't be faked: retention (people coming back to use it) and payment.

**[Evergreen]** The philosophical backbone is Paul Graham's essay [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html): "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them." Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York recruiting hosts and photographing apartments. Stripe's Collison brothers, when someone agreed to try Stripe, said "give me your laptop" and installed it on the spot — YC named it the **"Collison installation."** Graham's diagnosis of why founders resist: shyness plus laziness — they'd "rather sit at home writing code than go out and talk to a bunch of strangers." And they underestimate compounding. Graham's arithmetic in the essay: start with just 100 hand-recruited users and grow 10% a week, and a year later (1.1^52 ≈ 142x) you have ~14,000 users; two years later, 2 million. (Start from a literal handful of 5-10 and the same rate gets you ~700-1,400 in a year — still enough to prove the compounding engine works, which is the point.) Manual recruitment isn't a hack you outgrow; it's how you learn what to build. (See `09-networks-and-social-capital.md` for the relationship side of this.)

## 8. When is discovery DONE? Evidence thresholds that justify building

**[Evergreen]** Discovery never fully ends (Torres: weekly touchpoints forever), but the *decision to build seriously* deserves explicit thresholds. A defensible bar, synthesized from the sources above:

**For a B2B idea, build when you have:**
1. 30+ interviews in a *tight* segment where the last 5-10 produced no surprises;
2. The same top-1 pain named unprompted by a majority, with a quantified cost (hours or dollars);
3. Evidence of existing workarounds (they've *tried* to solve it);
4. 3-5 committed design partners, at least one signed LOI with a number on it, or ideally one paid pilot;
5. You've met at least a few economic buyers (not just users) and can describe the buying process from memory.

**For a consumer idea, build when you have:**
1. 20+ interviews showing a repeated, emotionally-charged pain or desire;
2. A landing-page/smoke test with conversion meaningfully above your baseline across 200+ cold visitors;
3. A waitlist whose members respond to emails (dead waitlists are vanity metrics);
4. Ideally, pre-orders or a concierge cohort that *retains* week over week — the same people keep showing up and paying.

**[Evergreen]** After launch, the discovery question becomes a measurement question. The standard instrument is the **Sean Ellis test**. Ellis is the growth practitioner who ran early growth at Dropbox, LogMeIn, and Eventbrite and coined the term "growth hacking." He derived the benchmark below by surveying users at ~100 startups and seeing which ones went on to succeed. Rahul Vohra — founder of Superhuman, the email startup — operationalized it: survey active users with "How would you feel if you could no longer use the product?" **40%+ answering "very disappointed"** is the classic threshold for product-market fit (**PMF** — the state where a market actively pulls the product out of you). Superhuman went from 22% → 33% → 58% by segmenting for high-expectation customers and splitting the roadmap 50/50 between doubling down on what fans loved and fixing what blocked fence-sitters ([How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit — First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)). And Todd Jackson's memorable ceiling check: **"If you have to ask whether you have product-market fit, then you don't"** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea)) — real PMF feels like trouble keeping up with demand. For how PMF evidence feeds into fundraising, see `05-fundraising.md`; for how it feeds idea selection, see `01-idea-evaluation.md`.

Equally important — **when discovery says STOP:** lukewarm "nice-to-have" reactions across 20+ interviews, no existing workarounds, nobody willing to commit even time, and a smoke test under 1% with decent copy. Killing an idea in week 4 is a *win* — it's exactly what this process is for, and it costs you $1,000 instead of two years.

## 9. Discovery anti-patterns

**[Evergreen]** All of these feel productive while destroying your data:

1. **Pitching in interviews.** Migicovsky's #1 error: the interview becomes a sales call, and every answer becomes polite defense-management. Pitch at the *end*, if at all, and only in solution-validation interviews.
2. **Leading questions.** "Don't you find X annoying?" manufactures agreement. Open with "what's the hardest part of…" and let them fill the space.
3. **Friends-and-family bias.** People who love you are structurally incapable of giving you bad news about your idea — that's literally why the book is called *The Mom Test*. Friends are fine for *practice* interviews and intros; their opinions are not evidence. Jackson's flag: payment counts most from "people you don't have a direct connection to."
4. **Logging compliments as validation.** Keep a compliment count; the number of "cool idea!"s in your notes should be zero.
5. **Hearing "yes" in a "maybe."** "Send me a deck," "loop back next quarter," "I'd definitely check it out" — all no's. Only commitments count.
6. **Talking to whoever's easiest** instead of the segment you'd actually sell to. 30 interviews with the wrong people validate nothing.
7. **Doing interviews solo and trusting memory.** Record (with permission) or bring a note-taker; we all remember the flattering half. Torres's fix: the people *building* the product don't decide anything based on secondhand summaries — they attend the interviews themselves.
8. **Asking users to design the product.** "What features do you want?" outsources your job. Collect problems; you own solutions.
9. **Stopping after the phase feels done.** Discovery is a habit, not a checkbox (Torres).

## 10. Running discovery in 2026

**[2026]** What's changed and what hasn't:

- **AI is a superb synthesis layer.** Recording tools transcribe every interview. A good LLM (large language model — the AI behind tools like ChatGPT and Claude) pass over 30 transcripts will extract recurring pains, count how often each was mentioned unprompted, pull exact quotes, and flag contradictions — work that used to take a week of sticky notes. Teresa Torres's community actively discusses AI-assisted interviewing and synthesis, precisely because recruiting, scheduling, and synthesis overhead is why most teams fail to sustain weekly discovery ([Perspective AI on operationalizing continuous discovery, 2026](https://getperspective.ai/blog/continuous-discovery-habits-in-2026-operationalizing-teresa-torres-s-framework-with-ai-conversations)). Practical stack: record → transcribe → per-interview structured summary (pains, quotes, commitments made) → cross-interview rollup. Always spot-check the rollup against raw transcripts; LLMs smooth over the weird outliers that are often the insight.
- **AI-simulated users are NOT validation.** "Synthetic users" — LLM personas you interview instead of humans — are seductive and dangerous. Nielsen Norman Group's studies found synthetic responses trend toward best-case, linear, optimized answers, missing the messy context real humans provide, and that **"since AI loves to please, every idea is often seen as a good one"** — making concept validation with synthetic users "incredibly risky" ([NN/g: Synthetic Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/synthetic-users/), [NN/g: Evaluating AI-Simulated Behavior](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-simulations-studies/)). Simulations can capture the *direction* of human trends but not magnitude or variance, and they inherit demographic biases. Legitimate uses: drafting interview scripts, rehearsing your interviewing technique, generating hypotheses to test on humans. Illegitimate use: counting a chatbot's enthusiasm as evidence. Remember the whole point (§1): validation is costly signal from someone with something to lose. An LLM has nothing to lose.
- **Demos are cheap, so the validation bar moved.** Anyone can now **vibe-code** a working prototype in a weekend — "vibe coding" is 2025-26 slang for building software by describing what you want to an AI coding tool in plain English and letting it write the code, no traditional programming required. So prototypes impress less, and *commitments* matter more than ever. The hierarchy in §4 got steeper, not obsolete.
- **Outreach is noisier.** Everyone's cold email is AI-polished now; response rates reward genuine specificity and in-person presence even more than before.

## 11. Your 4-6 week discovery playbook

Ready to run on one candidate idea. Assumes ~15-20 hours/week alongside whatever else you're doing. (For choosing *which* candidate idea to run this on first, see `01-idea-evaluation.md`.) **[Evergreen]** The structure and interview targets below restate the benchmarks from §3 and §8; **[2026]** the response-rate and dollar figures are current-market estimates — treat them as planning numbers, not physics.

**Week 0 (prep, 2-3 days):**
- Write down, before any interviews: target segment (narrow!), the problem you *believe* exists, your top 3 riskiest assumptions, and what evidence would change your mind. This is your pre-registration — a written prediction made in advance, which prevents you from moving the goalposts later.
- Build a list of 50 target interviewees (2nd-degree network + communities + cold list). Draft outreach. Set up a simple tracking sheet: name, segment, date, top pains, quotes, commitment obtained.

**Weeks 1-2 (problem discovery):**
- Goal: **10-15 Script-A interviews** (no idea mentioned). Send 40+ outreach messages. **[2026]** Expect ~25-40% conversion from warm intros (introductions made by someone the target already knows and trusts) and 5-15% from cold outreach — consistent with the norms in §3. Cold rates drift as inboxes get noisier, so recalibrate against your own first 20 sends.
- Record and transcribe everything; end each interview asking for two referrals.
- End of week 2: AI-assisted synthesis pass, plus your own read of the raw transcripts. Checkpoint: Is one pain named unprompted by >50%? Have most people *tried* to solve it? If no → pivot the segment or the problem, or move to the next candidate idea. That's success, not failure.

**Weeks 3-4 (solution validation begins):**
- Build the cheapest possible artifact: a Figma mockup or AI-built demo (B2B), or a landing page plus a $300-500 ad test (consumer).
- Goal: **8-10 Script-B interviews**, prioritizing the most-pained people from weeks 1-2 plus fresh faces (to avoid contaminated goodwill). For B2B, get at least 3 conversations with economic buyers, not just users.
- Every interview ends with a real ask: design-partner slot, LOI, pre-order, paid pilot, or intro to the buyer.
- Consumer track in parallel: run the landing page test from §6; email every signup asking for a call.

**Weeks 5-6 (commitment harvest + decision):**
- Chase every ask to a definite yes or no. "Maybe" = no.
- **[Evergreen]** Targets by end of week 6 — B2B: 25-30 total interviews, 3-5 design partners or 1-2 LOIs/paid pilots. Consumer: 20+ interviews, smoke test ≥5% conversion (or a clear message/segment winner), a responsive waitlist, ideally first pre-orders or a retaining concierge cohort. (These are the §8 thresholds on a deadline.)
- Write a one-page verdict against your Week-0 pre-registration: evidence for, evidence against, decision (build / pivot / kill), and what would change it. Share it with the smartest skeptic you know.
- If the verdict is "build": don't stop interviewing. Convert to Torres-style continuous discovery — one customer conversation per week, minimum, forever.

**[2026]** Total cash cost: roughly $500-1,500 at current prices (incentive gift cards at $50-100 each per §3, $300-500 of ad spend per §6, plus recording/transcription tools). Compare that to the cost of six months building the wrong thing.
