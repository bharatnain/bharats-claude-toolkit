# Networks & Who Can Help You — how founders get access

**Why this matters to you:** Your friends who built successful startups were not just smarter or luckier. Almost every hard step of their journey — finding a co-founder, hiring the first engineer, landing the first customer, getting the first check — was unlocked by a *person they had access to*, not by a form they filled out. The startup world runs on warm introductions and reputation, and nobody tells newcomers this explicitly. The good news: access is buildable, it compounds fast, and 2026 is arguably the easiest era ever for a technical person to build it from a standing start — because demonstrating skill in public has never been cheaper. This document maps who the helpful people are, how to reach them without being a networking sleaze, and what the norms are (advisor equity, co-founder splits, intro etiquette). It ends with a concrete 90-day plan for NYC.

---

## 1. Why access compounds — the unfair truth about startups

**[Evergreen]** Nearly every scarce resource in a startup's early life is allocated through networks, not markets:

- **Fundraising.** Most seed investors heavily prioritize *warm intros* over cold applications. (A "warm intro" is an introduction from someone the investor already trusts — a mutual contact emails them saying "you should meet Bharat." Contacting a stranger directly, with no such intro, is a "cold email.") Cold outreach *can* work — see Section 3 — but the base rates are brutal. Cold emails to VCs (venture capitalists — professional startup investors) commonly see [~95% non-reply rates](https://founderscapital8.substack.com/p/cold-emails-to-vcs-have-a-95-non). A warm intro from a founder the investor backed gets read almost every time. See `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md` and `06-investor-landscape-and-accelerators.md` for the mechanics.
- **Hiring.** Your first 5–10 hires will almost certainly come from people you or your co-founder already know, or one hop away. Nobody great answers a job posting from an unknown 2-person company. They join because someone they trust vouched for you.
- **Early customers.** Especially in B2B (business-to-business — selling to companies rather than consumers), the first 10 customers usually come from the founders' personal networks or from intros by investors and advisors. See `08-talking-to-users.md`.
- **Information.** Which investors are actually writing checks right now, what valuations similar companies got, which "hot" startup is secretly struggling — this circulates in private founder group chats and dinners, not on the public internet.

**[Evergreen]** Here is the compounding mechanism. Each credible interaction produces vouching, and vouching is transitive: one respected founder who thinks you're good will introduce you to five more people, and each of them now starts from "a person I trust rates this guy." Chris Fralic, longtime partner at First Round Capital (a top seed-stage venture firm), puts it bluntly in [How to Become Insanely Well-Connected](https://review.firstround.com/how-to-become-insanely-well-connected/): *"You're only as good as the people you know."* He treats reputation as an asset you deliberately build and protect.

**[Evergreen]** The most extreme documented example of network compounding is the "PayPal Mafia" — the ~28 early PayPal founders and executives (Peter Thiel, Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman, Max Levchin, and others) who went on to found or fund Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube, Palantir, Yelp, and more. Per an analysis by [Data Driven VC](https://www.newsletter.datadrivenvc.io/p/beyond-the-paypal-mafia-how-alumni), this one alumni network has been involved in 575 companies as founders, investors, or board members, with exits totaling more than $430 billion. Thiel alone made 91 investments, Levchin 77, Hoffman 66. That is what "access" looks like at the limit: a group of ex-colleagues funding, hiring, and advising each other for 25 years.

Your friends who succeeded almost certainly had some slice of this: a boss who became their first angel investor, a college classmate who became their co-founder, an ex-colleague who became customer #1. **The question is not whether you need this — you do. It's how fast you can build your own version.**

## 2. The categories of people who actually help

Not all "connections" are equal. Here is who materially moves the needle, roughly in order of value per hour of their time:

### a) Experienced founder mentors **[Evergreen]**
People 2–10 years ahead of you who have raised money, hired, shipped, and possibly failed. They are the highest-value advisors because they recently faced your exact problems. They help with gut-checks on your idea, intros to their investors, and telling you what's normal — for example, "is this term sheet weird?" (A "term sheet" is the short document an investor sends proposing the terms of their investment: how much money, at what valuation, with what rights. It gets signed before the full legal paperwork.) You find mentors by being in the same rooms (Sections 3–4) and by being interesting enough that they *want* to help.

### b) Operator angels **[Evergreen principle / 2026 numbers]**
An "angel investor" is an individual (not a fund) who invests their own money, typically in $5k–$100k checks, at the earliest stage. An "operator angel" is an angel who is also a current or recent executive or early employee at a successful company — say, a Stripe engineering director who angel-invests on the side. They're doubly valuable: money *plus* domain expertise, and they can become or recruit your early customers and hires. In 2026, operator angels at AI-era winners (OpenAI, Anthropic, Ramp, Figma alumni, etc.) are a major force in pre-seed rounds.

A quick decoder, since these stage names come up constantly. Startups raise money in named rounds of increasing size. **Pre-seed** is the very first outside money — often just an idea and a demo. **Seed** is the next round, used to find "product-market fit" — the point where enough people demonstrably want, use, and would miss your product that growth stops feeling like pushing a boulder uphill. **Series A** is the first large institutional round, raised once something is demonstrably working. **Series B, C,** and onward are progressively later, bigger growth rounds. Full mechanics in `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md`. See also `06-investor-landscape-and-accelerators.md`.

### c) Accelerator partners **[Evergreen]**
Accelerators are fixed-term programs that invest a standard amount in a batch of startups — Y Combinator (YC) is the canonical one. They are institutionalized access: the partners have seen thousands of companies, and the batch plus alumni network is a pre-built mafia. YC's alumni network is arguably the single most valuable startup network in the world. Full treatment in `06-investor-landscape-and-accelerators.md`.

### d) Colleague and alumni networks — the "mafia" effect **[Evergreen]**
The PayPal pattern generalizes. An [Accel × Dealroom "founder factories" study](https://dealroom.co/reports/founder-factories-founder-journey) of 360+ European and Israeli unicorns (a "unicorn" is a private startup valued at $1B+) found their ex-employees had founded 2,000+ new startups. Of those, 55% were founded in the same city as the parent company, and 59% had already raised private funding — evidence that working at a great startup transmits both skills and network. The same pattern is documented for Uber, Stripe, Airbnb, and Palantir alumni in the US.

**Practical implication for you:** if you have ex-colleagues from any strong tech company — FAANG (Facebook/Apple/Amazon/Netflix/Google, i.e., big tech) or, better, a fast-growing startup — that is your starting network. Ex-colleagues are the best source of co-founders, first hires, and vouching, because they've seen you actually work. If your work history is thin here, one legitimate path is spending 1–2 years at a top AI-era startup precisely to acquire the mafia. Weigh that against the cost of delay (see `01-idea-evaluation-and-conviction.md`).

### e) University networks **[Evergreen]**
Your school's founder and investor alumni will answer your cold email at 5–10x the rate of strangers. Shared affiliation is a weak but real trust signal, and university entrepreneurship centers exist specifically to hand out access: mentor lists, demo days, small grants. Section 5 covers the NYC university ecosystem specifically.

### f) Online communities and public work **[2026]**
Twitter/X, GitHub, technical Discords, and communities like [AI Tinkerers](https://nyc.aitinkerers.org/) are now genuine "top-of-funnel" for access. (The "funnel" is the sales metaphor for how a wide pool of strangers gradually narrows into a small group of real relationships or customers. "Top-of-funnel" just means the wide entry point where new people first discover you.) The mechanism: investors and senior operators actively scout people who ship impressive demos in public. This is the biggest structural change versus your friends who started 10 years ago — a great public demo or writeup can now generate inbound interest that used to require years of in-person networking.

**[Evergreen]** A note on what does *not* help much: collectors of "connections" — people with 10,000 LinkedIn contacts and no vouching. Access is depth times trust, not contact count.

## 3. Building access from a standing start — without being a sleaze

The core insight, and it is deeply [Evergreen]: **the startup world's networking norm is inverted from what newcomers expect. You don't get access by asking for things. You get it by being interesting and useful, after which people offer things.**

### Do interesting work in public
Paul Graham (co-founder of Y Combinator, whose essays are the closest thing startups have to scripture) writes in [How to Do Great Work](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html) that *"most people who are very good at something are happy to talk about it with anyone who's genuinely interested"* — and that if the best people in your field are collected in one place, *"it's usually a good idea to visit for a while."* His advice on colleagues applies directly to network-building: *"Quality is more important than quantity in colleagues. It's better to have one or two great ones than a building full of pretty good ones,"* and *"work with people you want to become like, because you will."*

For a technical person in 2026 **[2026]**, "interesting work in public" concretely means:
- Ship small, impressive projects and post demos (video beats text) on X/Twitter and in community Slacks and Discords.
- Write short technical essays about what you learned building — genuine, specific, no thought-leadership fluff. One good "here's what I learned building X with agents" post is worth 50 coffee chats.
- Demo at meetups. AI Tinkerers' entire format is live demos — see Section 5. Demoing converts you from "audience member" to "person others approach."

This works because it lets busy people evaluate you asynchronously at zero cost to them. It is the polar opposite of "can I pick your brain?" — the phrase that marks you as a taker.

### The "give before you ask" norm **[Evergreen]**
Before asking anyone for anything, be useful: send them a customer lead, a candidate, a sharp piece of feedback on their product, a relevant paper. Fralic's version: *"actually doing what you say you're going to do will put you in the top quartile."* The bar for follow-through is embarrassingly low, so meeting it is a differentiator. A practical cadence that compounds: send brief monthly update emails to people who've helped you — what you shipped, what you learned, one specific ask or no ask at all. Investors and mentors consistently say update emails are how they decide who to keep helping. After months of updates, [intros start getting offered to you unprompted](https://founderscapital8.substack.com/p/cold-emails-to-vcs-have-a-95-non).

### Cold outreach that actually works on busy, successful people
Cold outreach is not spam if done right; some meaningful fraction of great startup relationships started cold. The mechanics, mostly from [Fralic's First Round Review piece](https://review.firstround.com/how-to-become-insanely-well-connected/) **[Evergreen]**:

1. **Make it short and specific.** 5–8 sentences. Who you are (one line, with your strongest credibility signal), why *them specifically* (evidence you know their work — specifics, not flattery), and one concrete, low-lift ask.
2. **Write a "self-contained forwardable email" (SCFE).** Fralic's term: an email so complete and short that the recipient can forward it to the right person without editing. Even a "no" can become a forward.
3. **Give them an out.** Fralic: *"If you give people an out, it makes them more likely to act."* "No need to respond if this isn't relevant" paradoxically raises response rates.
4. **Ask for advice or a reaction, not a favor.** "Would you be open to a 20-minute call? I'm building X and you solved this exact problem at Y" outperforms any ask for intros, money, or jobs. Earn the intro later. Never ask for it first.
5. **Don't fake familiarity.** Fralic: *"Just say you don't have a connection and make a compelling argument for why we should meet."*
6. **Attach proof of work.** For a technical founder, a 60-second demo link is the single highest-conversion attachment. It answers "is this person real?" instantly.
7. **Prefer a quick no.** Chasing lukewarm maybes wastes months. Fralic tracks his own email response rate (~80%, for a well-known VC). If yours is under 10%, your message or targeting is broken — fix the message, don't send more volume.

**[Evergreen] Intro etiquette you're expected to know:** the norm is the "double opt-in intro" — when someone offers to introduce you to a third party, they first ask that person's permission before connecting you. When *you* request an intro, send your connector a short forwardable blurb (the SCFE again) so they can make the intro in 30 seconds. And always report back to whoever made an intro ("we met, here's what happened, thank you") — connectors keep helping the people who close the loop.

### What being a sleaze looks like (avoid)
Mass-connecting on LinkedIn. Pitching in the first message. Asking famous people generic questions Google answers. Treating every conversation as extraction. Name-dropping people who wouldn't vouch for you. The community is small and memory is long — Fralic's rule that you should *"assume you're going to run into everyone again"* is literally true in NYC and SF tech.

## 4. Mentors and advisors — how to find them, and what the equity norms are

**[Evergreen]** Distinguish three relationships:
- **Mentor:** informal, unpaid, episodic. Most of the help you'll get is this. Never offer equity for it; offer gratitude, updates, and usefulness.
- **Advisor:** a formalized ongoing relationship — regular calls, intros, recruiting help — compensated with a small equity grant.
- **Board member / investor:** covered in `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md`.

**How you actually get mentors:** you don't ask "will you be my mentor?" That reads as an open-ended time commitment and gets declined. Instead: ask a specific question, get a useful answer, execute on it, report back with results, and repeat. After 3–4 cycles a mentorship exists without anyone naming it. Formalize into an advisor role only when someone is *already* helping repeatedly and you want to lock in meaningful ongoing commitment.

### Advisor equity norms **[Evergreen principle / 2026 numbers]**
The standard reference is the **FAST agreement** (Founder/Advisor Standard Template), a free plug-and-play contract from the [Founder Institute](https://fi.co/fast), used since 2011, so you don't need custom legal work. Its current (v3, released mid-2026) equity grid, by company stage and advisor engagement level:

| Engagement | Pre-seed | Seed | Series A |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard** (monthly meetings) | 0.50% | 0.25% | 0.10% |
| **Expert** (hands-on: intros, projects, recruiting) | 1.00% | 0.75% | 0.50% |

Note the FAST grid is on the generous end. Market data runs lower: [Carta's data (via Equity Matrix)](https://equitymatrix.io/blog/how-much-equity-for-advisors) shows the *median* pre-seed advisor grant is ~0.21%, and only ~10% of pre-seed advisors get 1%+. A sane rule of thumb: **0.1%–0.5% for most advisors, up to ~1% only for a truly hands-on expert at the idea stage** [2026].

**Vesting:** FAST uses 2-year vesting with a 3-month cliff **[Evergreen]**. "Vesting" means the equity is earned gradually over time rather than granted upfront. A "cliff" means nothing at all is earned until that initial period passes. The cliff is your escape hatch: if the advisor turns out to be useless, part ways within 3 months and they get zero.

**Worked example [2026]:** Your company has 10,000,000 shares outstanding. You grant an advisor 0.25% = 25,000 shares, vesting monthly over 24 months with a 3-month cliff. Each month after the cliff, 25,000 ÷ 24 ≈ 1,042 shares vest (with the first 3 months' worth, 3,125 shares, vesting at the cliff). If you part ways at month 10, they keep 10/24 of the grant ≈ 10,417 shares = 0.104% of the company. Now suppose your company eventually exits at $100M, and dilution has halved their stake to ~0.05%. ("Dilution" is how everyone's ownership *percentage* shrinks as the company creates new shares for later investors and employees — your share count stays the same, but the total pie grows.) That ~0.05% is $50,000 — real money for them, trivial for you. That asymmetry is why good advisors say yes to small grants.

**[Evergreen] Red flags:** anyone who *asks* you for an advisor grant before helping. Anyone wanting 2%+ "advisor" stakes. "Advisors" who want cash retainers pre-revenue. Brokers who want a percentage of money raised — investors view paid fundraising intermediaries at early stage as a serious negative signal.

## 5. The NYC map (and SF equivalents)

You're in NYC — long established as the #2 startup ecosystem in the US, a position it has held since at least the mid-2010s (Startup Genome's global rankings [currently place it #2 worldwide](https://startupgenome.com/ecosystems/new-york-city)). What *is* new since ~2023: NYC has become a genuine AI hub, with a culture that skews slightly more toward fintech, B2B, media, and applied AI than SF's frontier-model scene. Here is the map, verified as of mid-2026 **[2026]** — communities change fast, so re-verify before showing up:

**Communities and spaces:**
- **[AI Tinkerers NYC](https://nyc.aitinkerers.org/)** — the highest-density technical AI meetup in the city: multi-hour evening events built around live code demos, with a no-pitch norm, heavy with founders and engineers from top labs and startups. Events typically run roughly monthly and draw large crowds. Venues rotate around the city, so check the event page for the current location and RSVP early — events fill up. **This should be your first stop: attend twice, then apply to demo.**
- **[South Park Commons NYC](https://blog.southparkcommons.com/p/announcing-spc-nyc)** — SPC is a members' community for the "-1 to 0" phase (their term for the exploration stage *before* you commit to an idea). Founded in SF, it opened its NYC space at 650 Broadway in NoHo. As the founders put it, *"the best ideas cannot start in isolation. They require a collective."* They also run a Founder Fellowship with funding for members who commit to starting. Membership is selective; a strong technical explorer with public work is exactly their profile.
- **[Betaworks](https://www.betaworks.com/camp)** — a "startup studio"/VC in the Meatpacking District. (A startup studio — also called a venture studio — is a firm that builds companies in-house with its own staff and capital and then spins them out. That's different from a VC, which only invests in outside founders, and from an accelerator, which runs fixed-term programs for existing teams. Betaworks does a mix of all three.) Its themed "Camp" accelerator programs come with investment and residency in their space, and recent cohorts have centered on AI — [past Camps include "AI Camp: Agent Systems," and the Fall '25 theme was "Interfaces"](https://www.betaworks.com/camp). Check their Camp page for the current theme and investment terms, which change per cohort. Their events are also a good scene even if you don't do Camp.
- **[Antler NYC](https://elev-x.com/news-insights/article-antler-accelerator-review/)** — a "day zero" investor that runs residencies in NYC (among other cities) where solo people ideate and team up in person, then pitch Antler's committee for investment. Recent US terms have been in the $250k–$500k range, but terms have changed repeatedly — verify current numbers directly with Antler before applying. Useful especially if you need co-founder matching plus structure. See `06-investor-landscape-and-accelerators.md` for whether the dilution is worth it for someone with your profile.
- **[NY Tech Meetup / NY Tech Alliance](https://nytech.org/our_history)** — founded 2004 by Meetup's Scott Heiferman and Dawn Barber; ~50,000 members; monthly demo nights. This is the granddaddy of NYC tech community, and its lineage seeded much of the current scene. Today it's broader and less elite than AI Tinkerers, but demo slots are real exposure.
- **Tech:NYC** — the industry nonprofit connecting the NYC tech ecosystem (policy, events, ecosystem reports). Useful for ecosystem awareness more than personal access.
- **NY Tech Week** (June, run by a16z and others) — a week of hundreds of decentralized events, founder dinners, and demo days across the city. The single densest access-building week of the NYC calendar.
- **On Deck-style cohort communities** — [On Deck's Founder Fellowship (ODF)](https://joinodf.com/) is a remote ~10-week paid community for people exploring startups (1,000+ startups launched from it). Cohort communities are a legitimate shortcut to a peer network, though quality varies by cohort. SPC and Antler are the higher-selectivity versions of the same idea.
- **Founder dinners:** small invite-only dinners are where the real information flows in NYC. You don't find these; they find you, via everything else in this section. Hosting your own is an underrated hack — 6–8 people, a specific theme (e.g., "agents in production") — because the host gets the network.

**Universities [2026]:**
- **[Cornell Tech](https://tech.cornell.edu/impact/runway/)** (Roosevelt Island) — the most startup-oriented. Cornell Tech reports [~100 startups created, with roughly $250M raised](https://tech.cornell.edu/impact/) by them collectively. Its **Runway program** — a funded postdoc position for founders; no relation to the finance term "runway" in Section 6 — turns recent PhDs into startup CEOs with a package of salary, research budget, and company funding. [Runway companies alone have raised more than $315M in private funding and employ 790+ people](https://tech.cornell.edu/impact/runway/). Its Startup Studio feeds NYCEDC (NYC Economic Development Corporation) Founder Fellowships. Their public events and demo days are attendable.
- **Columbia** (entrepreneurship center + engineering) and **NYU** (Tandon in Brooklyn, plus Stern) both run active founder programs, hackathons, and mentor networks. **If you have any degree, activate your own school's alumni founder network first** — even a non-NYC school's NYC alumni chapter — because shared affiliation multiplies cold-outreach response rates.

**SF equivalents (if you raise or relocate there):** YC and its alumni network (Mountain View/SF; see `06`); South Park Commons' original SF space; AI Tinkerers SF; the "Cerebral Valley" hackathon-and-hacker-house scene in Hayes Valley; and a far higher ambient density of investors and frontier-lab people. **[2026]** The practical 2026 pattern for someone like you: build in NYC (it's now fully sufficient for pre-seed/seed, and NYC's applied-AI and enterprise customer density is an advantage), and fly to SF for 1–2 week sprints when fundraising or hiring frontier talent. Many NYC founders do exactly this.

## 6. Co-founders — the highest-stakes network decision

### Why it matters to investors **[Evergreen]**
Historically ~[10% of YC-funded companies are solo-founded](https://zyner.io/blog/yc-solo-founders). YC funds solo founders but openly prefers teams, both for workload and for the "bus factor" — if one person quits or burns out, a solo company dies. Investors read a strong co-founder as evidence you can convince at least one talented person to bet their career on you. YC's data-driven corollary, per Dalton Caldwell (longtime YC Managing Director): [companies lacking a technical co-founder underperform](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/why-you-really-do-need-a-technical-co-founder). As a technical person, *you are the scarce half of the pairing* — significant leverage. Don't add a "business co-founder" who only brings slides; at the earliest stage you can do the talking-to-users yourself (`08-talking-to-users.md`).

### Finding one **[2026 tools, Evergreen norms]**
- **Best source:** people you've already worked with — ex-colleagues, classmates, hackathon partners. Trust built under real work pressure is the thing you cannot shortcut.
- **[YC Co-Founder Matching](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/co-founder-matching/)** — a free, YC-vetted matching platform (deliberately built like a dating app) inside Startup School. It's now the world's largest, with 100,000+ matches and ~40,000 profiles; the median time from signup to meeting an eventual co-founder is ~100 days. YC built it because 20% of active Startup School founders reported seeking a co-founder, and 25% of aspiring founders cited lacking one as their blocker to starting.
- **Antler / SPC / hackathons** — in-person, work-together-first venues (Section 5).

**Co-founder dating norms [Evergreen]:** the accepted process is to *trial-work before committing* — a 2–8 week project sprint together, plus explicit conversations about equity, roles, money runway, personal ambition, and quit-conditions. ("Runway" here is the finance term: how many months you can survive on the cash you have before running out. For pre-idea founders, it means how long each of you can afford to live without a salary. It's unrelated to Cornell Tech's Runway program in Section 5.) YC encourages matched pairs to do trial projects for exactly this reason. Most trials should end in "no" — that's the system working.

### Equity splits and vesting **[Evergreen]**
Michael Seibel (YC partner, co-founder of Twitch/Justin.tv) makes the canonical argument in [How to split equity among founders](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-split-equity-among-founders): default to **equal or near-equal splits**. His reasoning: *"It takes 7 to 10 years to build a company of great value. Small variations in year one do not justify massively different founder equity splits in year 2-10."* Unequal splits demotivate the junior partner for a decade-long journey, and they signal to investors that the CEO doesn't really value the co-founder. Execution beats the idea — don't award a big premium for "it was my idea."

**Vesting is non-negotiable:** the standard is **4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff** for founders. Yes, founders vest their own stock — this protects each founder from the other leaving early with a huge stake. Seibel's point: *because* vesting protects you, you can afford to be generous with the split.

**Worked example [Evergreen]:** You and a co-founder split 50/50 — each holds 5,000,000 of 10,000,000 shares, with 4-year vesting and a 1-year cliff. If she leaves at month 10 (before the cliff), she keeps 0 shares. The company (you) keeps everything, and you can grant that equity to her replacement. If she leaves at month 30, she keeps 30/48 = 62.5% of her grant = 3,125,000 shares (31.25% of the company), and the unvested 1,875,000 shares return to the company. Compare the no-vesting nightmare: she leaves at month 10 owning 50% forever — a "dead equity" cap table that most investors will simply refuse to fund. ("Cap table" = the capitalization table, the ledger of who owns what.) This single clause is the difference between a survivable co-founder breakup and a dead company — co-founder conflict is a leading startup killer (see `11-failure-postmortems.md`).

## 7. Whose advice should you trust? A filter

You're about to receive an enormous amount of confident, contradictory advice. Filters, all **[Evergreen]**:

1. **Stage-relevance.** Advice is only reliable from people ~1–2 stages ahead of you, or from people (like YC partners) who see hundreds of companies at your stage. A Series C executive's advice about process and management is actively harmful at pre-seed. Ask: "when did you last do the thing I'm asking about, at my stage?"
2. **Incentives.** Investors advise you to raise more. Accelerators advise you to accelerate. Agencies advise you to outsource. Always ask what the advisor gains if you follow the advice. This doesn't make them liars; it makes them lens-distorted.
3. **Survivorship bias.** Successful founders explain their success with the visible things they did — but thousands did the same things and failed. Treat any single founder's story as one sample, not a law. Aggregate advice (YC's essays and data, First Round Review's interview corpus, Carta's datasets) beats anecdote because it's built from many samples, including failures.
4. **Advice is autobiographical.** People recommend the path that worked *for them, in their year, in their market*. A 2015 SaaS playbook may be wrong for 2026 AI products. (SaaS = "software as a service" — software sold as a recurring subscription rather than a one-time purchase, the dominant software business model of the 2010s.) Extract the *reasoning*, discard the surface prescription. See `10-ai-era-startups.md`.
5. **Watch for the disagreement pattern.** When ~everyone credible at your stage agrees (e.g., "talk to users," "vest founder stock"), treat it as settled. When credible people split (e.g., "raise big early" vs. "stay lean"), the answer is situational — figure out which situation you're in instead of picking a camp.

## 8. Your 90-day access-building plan (NYC, technical founder, 2026)

**[2026]** Assumes ~10–15 hours/week alongside building. The plan's engine is Section 3: public work → rooms → usefulness → vouching.

**Days 1–30: Establish a public surface.**
- Write a one-paragraph "what I'm exploring" statement (see `01-idea-evaluation-and-conviction.md` for choosing directions). You need a crisp answer to "what are you working on?" even pre-idea: "I'm technical, exploring X and Y, currently building Z to learn."
- Ship one small, impressive demo in your candidate direction. Post a 60-second video plus a short writeup on X/Twitter and LinkedIn. Repeat every ~2 weeks — cadence matters more than polish.
- Sign up for [Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/) (free). Browse — but don't yet commit to — co-founder matching, to calibrate who's out there.
- Attend one AI Tinkerers NYC event and one NY Tech Meetup demo night. Talk to 3 people at each; the goal is names-and-follow-ups, not volume. Send a same-day follow-up note to each, containing something useful.
- List 20 people you already know who are 1–2 degrees from startups (ex-colleagues, classmates, that friend who raised). Tell each what you're exploring and ask one specific question. This "reactivation pass" is the highest-ROI single action in this plan — these people already trust you.

**Days 31–60: Convert audience-member to participant.**
- Apply to demo at AI Tinkerers (a working agent demo clears the bar). Demoing flips the dynamic: people now approach you.
- Apply to South Park Commons NYC. Look at Betaworks' current Camp theme and Antler NYC dates as fits for your direction (weigh terms via `06-investor-landscape-and-accelerators.md`).
- Run a cold outreach sprint: 15 carefully researched SCFE-style emails (Section 3) to NYC founders and operator angels in your candidate space — advice-asks with your demo attached, never money-asks. Set expectations honestly: there's no reliable published response-rate benchmark for this kind of outreach, but well-researched advice-asks to founders do far better than the [~95% non-reply rate of cold money-asks to VCs](https://founderscapital8.substack.com/p/cold-emails-to-vcs-have-a-95-non) (Section 1). If 3–5 of the 15 turn into real conversations, the sprint worked. If you get near-zero replies, fix the message and targeting rather than sending more volume (Section 3, point 7).
- Start a monthly update email to everyone who has helped so far (even 10 recipients). What you built, what you learned, one ask.
- Activate your university's NYC alumni founder network. Attend one Cornell Tech / Columbia / NYU public startup event.

**Days 61–90: Deepen and formalize.**
- From your conversations, identify 2–3 people whose advice was concretely useful. Go back with results: "you suggested X, I did it, here's what happened." This is how mentors form.
- If a candidate direction has hardened, begin deliberate co-founder exploration: put your YC matching profile live, and send trial-project invitations to the 2–3 best builders you've met (Section 6 norms).
- Host one small dinner or build-night — 6–8 of the best people you've met, one theme. You are now a node, not just an edge.
- Checkpoint metrics: ~5 people who'd take your call within a day; ≥2 proto-mentors; ≥1 trial co-founder conversation; 1 public demo with real engagement; an update list that gets replies. If you're at half of this, you're on track — compounding is slow for 90 days and then startlingly fast.

The meta-rule, once more, because everything above is a special case of it **[Evergreen]**: *make things people find impressive, be reliably useful, and close every loop.* Access follows.
