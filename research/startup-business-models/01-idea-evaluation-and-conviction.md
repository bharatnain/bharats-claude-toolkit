# Idea Evaluation & How Founders Reach Conviction

**Why this matters to you:** You are pre-idea. That feels like a disadvantage. It is actually the one moment when rigor is free: killing an idea today costs you nothing, while killing it in year two costs you your savings, your reputation with early hires, and two years of compounding. The founders you've watched succeed did not get lucky with a flash of inspiration. Almost all of them ran a repeatable, mostly unglamorous evaluation process. The "certainty" you saw from the outside was manufactured out of evidence, piece by piece. This document explains how great ideas are actually found; the exact checklists that partners at Y Combinator (YC — the famous startup accelerator, an organization that funds and coaches very early companies) and top angel investors use to score ideas; how to answer "why now"; how to know when an idea is dead; and how to climb from hunch to conviction with your own candidate directions. Read it before you write a line of code.

---

## 1. How great startup ideas are actually found

### Organic vs. manufactured ideas

**[Evergreen]** The single most-cited piece of writing on this topic is Paul Graham's essay [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html). (Graham co-founded Y Combinator.) Its opening line is the whole thesis:

> "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas."

Graham distinguishes **organic ideas** — problems you personally have and notice — from **made-up ideas** — things you brainstormed because they "sound like a startup." Made-up ideas fail in a characteristic way: they produce products that many people are *mildly* interested in and nobody urgently needs. Graham's test: name the specific people who need this so badly they'll use a broken v1. "If you can't answer that, the idea is probably bad."

> "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not 'think up' but 'notice.'"

**[Evergreen]** Important nuance: "organic" does not mean you must passively wait for lightning. YC partner Jared Friedman's Startup School lecture [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas) says explicitly that manufactured ideas can work *if you generate them systematically*. Systematic starting points: a founder's unfair advantage; a promising wave (in 2026: applied AI); problems you've seen at work; or importing a proven model into a new market (Rappi = DoorDash for Latin America). What fails is the shallow version: sitting in a room inventing plausible-sounding apps. One more reason not to obsess over the first idea: of YC's top 100 companies, roughly a quarter **pivoted** — changed to a totally different idea while keeping the company and team — after starting. So choosing a rich *problem space* matters more than nailing the first idea (per Friedman's talk, summarized in [these notes](https://gist.github.com/crkrenn/3ab4024df9da10c0b9dc7379c36358e2)).

### "Live in the future"

**[Evergreen]** Graham's recipe for becoming the kind of person who notices billion-dollar ideas:

> "Live in the future, then build what's missing."

Be at the leading edge of a rapidly changing field — as a *user*, not a spectator. The missing pieces then become obvious to you before they're obvious to everyone else. "Coming up with startup ideas is a question of seeing the obvious" — obvious to you because of where you're standing. **[2026]** For you, concretely: the fastest-moving frontier right now is AI agents, coding models, and AI-native workflows. If your candidate directions are AI-adjacent, "living in the future" means using the newest models and tools daily, hitting their limits, and writing down every point where you think "why doesn't this exist yet."

**[Evergreen]** Corollary: worrying that you're late is a *good* sign. Graham: "Worrying that you're late is one of the signs of a good idea." Dismissal is a good sign too: "When something is described as a toy, that means it has everything an idea needs except being important."

### Schlep blindness — where the good ideas hide

**[Evergreen]** A "schlep" is a Yiddish-derived word for a tedious, unpleasant task. In [Schlep Blindness](https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html), Graham argues that your unconscious filters out ideas that involve painful work — sales grind, regulatory mess, dealing with banks — before you even consciously consider them:

> "Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve painful schleps. That's schlep blindness."

His canonical example is Stripe (online payments infrastructure): "For over a decade, every hacker who'd ever had to process payments online knew how painful the experience was. Thousands of people must have known about this problem." The idea sat in plain sight because it looked like a slog. The payoff for overcoming this bias: "If you pick an ambitious idea, you'll have less competition, because everyone else will have been frightened off." A practical trick from the same essay: instead of asking "what problem should I solve?", ask **"what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?"**

### Tarpit ideas — where the bad ideas hide

**[Evergreen]** The mirror image, from Jared Friedman: a **tarpit idea** looks appealing, seems easy, and has attracted (and killed) waves of founders for years — because a hidden structural reason keeps it from working. Classic examples: an app to coordinate plans with friends, an app to discover bars/restaurants/events, generic photo-sharing. These feel validated ("everyone I ask says they'd use it!") precisely because the problem is widely *felt*. But it's not urgently felt, not monetizable, or structurally blocked. (Discovery apps, for instance, die because people use them too rarely to keep coming back.) Friedman's advice if you're drawn to one: Google it, find the corpses, talk to founders who tried it, and only proceed if you have a genuinely novel answer for why they all failed. **[2026]** The current tarpit list has new entries: thin "AI wrapper" apps (a minimal interface over someone else's model), generic AI chatbots, AI journaling/companion apps, and "ChatGPT for X" where X is something the model providers will ship natively. Current analyses estimate that the large majority of thin wrappers fail or never reach meaningful revenue ([Moat test for AI wrappers, 2026](https://preuve.ai/blog/are-ai-wrapper-startups-worth-building-2026)); treat those specific failure-rate numbers as directional, not audited.

## 2. The evaluation lens: what YC and top investors actually score

### A startup idea is a growth hypothesis

**[Evergreen]** YC partner Kevin Hale's lecture [How to Evaluate Startup Ideas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DOtCl5PU8F0) reframes the whole exercise. A startup idea is not a product description. It is **a hypothesis about why this company will grow very fast**. The hypothesis has three parts: the **problem** (the conditions that make growth possible), the **solution** (the mechanism), and the **insight** (your unfair advantage — why *you*, why *this* wins). You evaluate an idea by stress-testing each part.

Hale's checklist for a great problem (summarized [here](https://youngbusinesshub.org/how-to-evaluate-start-up-ideas/)) — an ideal problem is:

- **Popular** — many people have it (his rough bar: millions of users, or tens of thousands of businesses).
- **Growing** — the underlying market grows ~20%+ per year, so the market's growth pulls you along.
- **Urgent** — people want it solved *now* ("hair on fire": if your hair is on fire and someone sells you a brick, you'll use the brick).
- **Expensive** — solving it is worth real money, so you can charge.
- **Mandatory** — regulation or necessity forces people to deal with it (e.g., payroll, compliance).
- **Frequent** — it recurs often, ideally daily or hourly. Hale flags frequency as the most important trait: frequent problems create frequent opportunities to convert intent into usage.

No idea hits all six. A great one hits "growing + urgent + frequent" or "expensive + mandatory."

The insight side — your unfair advantage — must be one of five kinds. **Founder** advantage: you're one of a handful of people in the world with this expertise. **Market** advantage: you're riding 20%+/yr growth. **Product** advantage: your product is **10x** better or cheaper than alternatives — and it must genuinely be an order of magnitude, not 2x, because 2x isn't enough to overcome customers' inertia against switching. **Acquisition** advantage: the product grows by word of mouth, for free. **Monopoly** advantage: network effects — the product gets better as more people use it — make you harder to displace as you grow.

### Friedman's 10 questions

**[Evergreen]** From the same YC Startup School series, Jared Friedman's explicit evaluation checklist ([notes](https://gist.github.com/crkrenn/3ab4024df9da10c0b9dc7379c36358e2)):

1. **Do you have founder-market fit?** — Are you among the best-placed people to work on this? (You'll hear this term constantly. It means the match between your specific skills and experience and this specific problem. Brex — corporate credit cards for startups — worked partly because the founders had already built a payments company.)
2. **How big is the market?** — Either big now, or small but growing fast. The classic example: Coinbase started in a tiny market growing explosively.
3. **How acute is the problem?** — Severity, not just breadth.
4. **Do you have competition?** — Counterintuitively, some competition validates the market; you then need a new insight. Dropbox was roughly the 20th file-sync product.
5. **Do you want this yourself?** / Do you know people who do?
6. **Did this only recently become possible or necessary?** — the "why now" (below).
7. **Are there good proxies?** — successful companies doing something similar in an adjacent market or geography.
8. **Would you want to work on this for years?** — Passion can grow with **traction**. (Traction is core startup jargon: measurable evidence that people actually want what you're building — user numbers, usage, or revenue that are real and growing, as opposed to plans, praise, or projections.) But check yourself for active dread.
9. **Is this a scalable business?** — Software scales; heavy human services often don't.
10. **Is this a good idea *space*?** — a class of ideas with plausible hits, since you'll likely pivot within the space.

His three **counterintuitive signs of a good idea**: it's *hard to get started* (which scares others off — the schlep again); it's in a *boring space* (payroll, compliance, logistics — less competition per unit of opportunity); and it *has existing competitors* (proof of demand).

### Thiel's contrarian question and "secrets"

**[Evergreen]** Peter Thiel (PayPal co-founder, first outside investor in Facebook) built his book *Zero to One* around one interview question: **"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"** ([summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/peter-thiel-contrarian-question/)). In his framing, every great company is built on a **secret** — an important truth that is knowable but not yet widely believed. A bad answer is merely controversial-but-common ("education is broken"). A good answer is specific, defensible, and makes the room go quiet. Why this matters for evaluation: if your idea contains no secret — if everyone agrees it's a good idea — then either many teams are already doing it, or the market has correctly judged it as bad. So your evaluation question becomes: *what do I know or believe, from lived experience, that the consensus rejects — and what evidence do I have that I'm right and they're wrong?* Airbnb's secret was "strangers will sleep in each other's homes." Figma's was "professional-grade design tools can run in a browser, and design should be multiplayer." **[2026]** In AI, the consensus moves so fast that secrets have short half-lives. The durable ones tend to be about *specific industries' workflows and data* (things model labs can't see), not about model capabilities (things everyone sees within weeks of a release).

### What famous angels screen for: Naval Ravikant and Elad Gil

**[Evergreen]** First, the jargon. An **angel investor** ("angel") is an individual who invests their *own* money in startups at the very earliest stage — typically checks from ~$10K to a few hundred thousand dollars, often before a product exists. Contrast a **venture capitalist (VC)**, who invests a fund raised from institutions. Because angels commit before much evidence exists, their checklists lean harder on the *founder* and on a single exceptional signal than YC's problem-centric lists do. Two of the most-studied angels have made their criteria explicit:

**Naval Ravikant** co-founded AngelList (the platform where startups raise money from angels) and is one of the most successful angels ever: early checks into Uber, Twitter, Notion, and Postmates, with 16 of his portfolio companies (companies he invested in) becoming unicorns — startups valued at $1B+ ([Hustle Fund's breakdown](https://www.hustlefund.vc/post/angel-squad-naval-ravikant-investments-the-angel-investors-angel-investor)). His talk [The Anatomy of a Fundable Startup](https://fi.co/insight/the-anatomy-of-a-fundable-startup-by-naval-ravikant) lays out the five things investors actually score — **traction, team, product, social proof, and pitch**, roughly in that priority order — plus a meta-rule that matters more than the list itself: "Investors are looking for something exceptional about the company. Instead of trying to do everything well... do one thing exceptional." A B+ on all five loses to an A++ on one, because angels are hunting outliers, not solid citizens. On the team specifically, his filter is the triad he applies to any business partner: high **intelligence, energy, and, above all, integrity** ([summary of Naval's lessons](https://visible.vc/blog/7-lessons-for-entrepreneurs-from-naval-ravikant/)). Also instructive: his own picks cluster overwhelmingly in *tools for builders* — engineers, developers, operators — because that was the world he lived in. His domain expertise compressed his risk. That is the investor-side mirror of founder-market fit.

**Elad Gil** is an ex-Google/Twitter operator turned prolific solo angel (later solo VC) — an early investor in Airbnb, Stripe, Coinbase, Square, Instacart, and about 40 other unicorns (40+ in total) — and the author of the *High Growth Handbook*. His screen runs market-first, then founder, then timing ([Hustle Fund's breakdown](https://www.hustlefund.vc/post/angel-squad-elad-gil-investments-lessons-from-backing-40-unicorns-on-founder-quality-and-market-timing)):

1. **Market size and dynamics.** Is the market genuinely large enough to support multiple successful companies, or is it winner-take-all? He runs a blunt personal-use test: would *you* pay for this product? Would your friends? Would an enterprise?
2. **Founder execution over pedigree.** Has this founder actually *shipped* something — not just inside a big company, but independently? Can they articulate exactly why customers buy and what alternatives customers are replacing? He weights adaptability to changing conditions over vision alone.
3. **Timing.** Is the market reaching an inflection point where adoption is about to accelerate? This is his version of the "why now" question (section 3).
4. **Signals of pull:** weekly product pushes (velocity); A-plus people choosing to join early (magnetic hiring); and real engagement rather than **vanity metrics** — numbers that look impressive but don't prove anyone values the product, like cumulative sign-ups, app downloads, or page views. The non-vanity versions are the ones that can go *down* if the product stops being useful: active users, retention (people coming back), and revenue.

**[Evergreen]** Notice the convergence. Naval's "one thing exceptional" rhymes with Hale's insight/unfair-advantage test. Gil's inflection point *is* the why-now. Both echo Friedman's founder-market fit. When YC partners and famous angels arrive at the same handful of questions from different directions, treat those questions as the canon — and pre-answer them for your own candidate ideas before anyone else asks.

### The "would customers cancel?" test for AI ideas

**[2026]** The current-era addition to every checklist: **if a foundation model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) shipped your product as a default feature next quarter, would your customers cancel?** If yes, you're building a feature, not a company ([AI wrapper moat test](https://preuve.ai/blog/are-ai-wrapper-startups-worth-building-2026)). Durable AI-era advantages look like: proprietary data that compounds with usage; deep workflow integration with real switching costs; distribution into a channel horizontal tools can't reach; or orchestration complexity well above the base model. **[2026]** Equally important is YC's counterweight to moat-obsession. Before **product-market fit** (PMF — the milestone where your product satisfies a real market so well that demand starts pulling you: users stick around, refer others, and growth stops feeling forced; before it, you are pushing the product uphill), your only real defense is speed. "A moat is not a starting point, it's a result" (Jared Friedman, quoted in [YC's real moats for AI startups](https://medium.com/startup-insider-edge/forget-proprietary-models-y-combinators-7-real-moats-for-ai-startups-00c02fdd5966)). Evaluate for a *plausible eventual* moat, then win by moving faster than everyone.

## 3. "Why now," in depth

**[Evergreen]** Sequoia Capital is one of the oldest and most successful venture capital firms in the world — an early backer of Apple, Google, Stripe, and WhatsApp. That is why its famous pitch-deck template is treated as a de facto industry standard. The template dedicates an entire early slide to one question: **why is now the moment this becomes possible or inevitable?** ([Sequoia template](https://slidebean.com/templates/sequoia-pitch-deck-template)). Investors weight this heavily because most startup failures are timing failures wearing a product-failure costume. The logic cuts both ways. If you can't answer "why now," you must explain why this wasn't built five years ago (usually: it was, and it died) and why it won't be commoditized five years from now ([why "why now" is underrated](https://www.startupfolsom.org/blog/funnel-fail-makeover-4a32h-hck5n-derac-aflfr)).

A real "why now" is a recent, specific **unlock**, in one of four categories:

1. **Technology unlock** — a capability crossed a threshold. Figma: WebGL — a browser API (an interface that lets software talk to other software) for GPU-accelerated graphics — made professional design tools possible in a browser for the first time (~2012). Uber: smartphones with GPS in every pocket.
2. **Economic unlock** — a cost collapsed. Cloud computing collapsing infrastructure cost enabled the entire SaaS wave (Software-as-a-Service — software sold as a subscription over the internet). **[2026]** LLM (large language model) inference cost per token has been falling steeply year over year, which keeps re-pricing which AI products are viable. An idea that was uneconomic at 2023 prices may be trivially profitable now.
3. **Regulatory/structural unlock** — a law or platform shift. Checkr (background checks via API) became necessary when gig platforms needed to screen millions of workers programmatically (a Friedman example).
4. **Behavioral unlock** — people changed. COVID normalizing remote work unlocked a decade of collaboration tools in two years. Airbnb needed a generation comfortable with online strangers (eBay and Couchsurfing had softened the ground) plus a recession that made hosts need the money.

**[Evergreen]** The test of a good "why now": it should imply a *window*. Too early, and you educate the market and die (dozens of pre-2007 "smartphone" plays). Too late, and you face incumbents or a swarm of competitors. The best answers are 12–36 months after the unlock, when the capability is real but the market hasn't consolidated. **[2026]** The AI-era catch: the unlock "LLMs got good" is now *everyone's* why-now, so it no longer differentiates. You need a second-order version: "LLMs got good **and** [specific vertical] has [specific painful workflow] with [specific data/distribution] that generalist tools can't touch."

## 4. Kill criteria — deciding an idea is dead

**[Evergreen]** The discipline that separates systematic founders from wandering ones: **write down, in advance, what result would make you drop the idea.** Decided in advance, it's a kill criterion. Decided after the fact, it's a rationalization. Kill signals with strong consensus behind them:

- **Universal politeness.** Everyone says "cool, I'd try that" and nobody does anything. Todd Jackson — a partner at First Round Capital (a prominent early-stage venture firm), ex-Google/Facebook — calls lukewarm interest a trap: real validation "feels undeniable rather than ambiguous" ([How to validate your startup idea](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea)).
- **No pull after ~30 real conversations.** Jackson's benchmark: roughly 20–30 interviews for consumer ideas, 50+ for B2B (business-to-business — selling to companies), continued "until clear patterns emerge." If after that volume you cannot find a cluster of people who have the problem *urgently*, kill or reshape.
- **You can't name the first 10 customers.** Graham's test from [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html): no identifiable desperate users, no idea.
- **The tarpit graveyard check fails.** You find five dead startups that tried exactly this, and your explanation of why you're different is "we'll execute better." That is not an insight.
- **Zero willingness to pre-commit.** Nobody will pay a deposit, sign a design-partner agreement (an arrangement where an early customer commits time and feedback in exchange for influence and discounts), or even schedule a second meeting. Money and calendar time are the only honest currencies.
- **The economics can't work even in the best case.** Do the arithmetic *before* building. Worked example: suppose your idea is an AI meeting-prep tool for salespeople at $30/month. If realistic reachable buyers are ~500k sales reps in your channel, and a very good outcome is 2% penetration (winning 2% of them), that's 10,000 × $360/yr = **$3.6M ARR** (Annual Recurring Revenue — your subscription revenue expressed as a yearly rate) at *success*. That's a nice business but cannot be a venture-scale one — venture capitalists need credible paths to $100M+ ARR (see `05-fundraising.md`). So either the wedge must expand, or the idea is dead *as a venture bet* while alive as a **lifestyle business**. (A **wedge** is the deliberately narrow first product or market you enter — the thin end that gets you in, from which you expand into the bigger opportunity. A lifestyle business is one run to provide its founders a good income rather than to grow to venture scale — a perfectly fine outcome, just a different game with different funding.) Killing an idea for the wrong *goal* is also a kill.
- **A structural dependency you can't control.** Your whole business depends on platform access — an API, an app store, a data source — that the owner has both the ability and the incentive to cut off. **[2026]** This is the wrapper problem in its general form.

**[Evergreen]** What is *not* a kill signal: the work looks hard (that's the schlep — a feature, not a bug); competitors exist (validation); smart people are skeptical (Graham on Airbnb: "We didn't even like the idea that much" — [The Airbnbs](https://paulgraham.com/airbnbs.html)); or you're bored after two weeks (check for dread, not for novelty wearing off). Also: killing the idea rarely means killing the *space*. Friedman's data point — ~25% of top YC companies pivoted entirely — means the standard move is to stay in the space, keep the learnings, and change the wedge.

## 5. Building conviction: empirical vs. analytical

**[Evergreen]** There are two ways to convince yourself, and successful founders use both — in a specific ratio: **analysis to choose the space, empiricism to choose the idea.**

### The analytical layer (cheap, fast, weak evidence)

Market sizing, competitive mapping, reading industry reports, talking to investors. Necessary — this is where Hale's six problem qualities and the venture-scale arithmetic live — but *never sufficient*. Analysis operates on public information, and (per Thiel) public information contains no secrets. A spreadsheet can prove an idea is *dead*; it cannot prove an idea is *alive*.

### The empirical layer (expensive, slow, strong evidence)

This is where conviction actually comes from, in ascending order of strength:

1. **Problem interviews.** ~30 conversations (Jackson's number) asking about *past behavior*, never hypotheticals. Not "would you use X?" but "how have you tried to solve this before, and what did it cost you?" (Full technique: see `08-talking-to-users.md`.)
2. **Smoke tests / waitlists.** A landing page describing the product plus a sign-up form. Weak-to-moderate signal — it measures attention, not commitment — but the arithmetic is useful. If you drive 2,000 relevant visitors and get a 1% email signup rate (20 people), that's noise. 10%+ (200 people), with unsolicited "when can I get this?" replies, is signal. Ryan Petersen validated Flexport (freight forwarding software) with a landing page for a not-yet-real product — over 300 companies signed up ([via Jackson](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea)).
3. **Prototypes / MVTs.** Gagan Biyani (co-founder of Udemy and Maven) argues for the **Minimum Viable Test** (MVT) over the MVP (Minimum Viable Product — the smallest complete version of a product). Don't simulate the whole car; test only the riskiest assumption. "In an MVP, you try to simulate the entire car. In an MVT, you are just testing whether the drivetrain is more powerful with an electric engine or a gas one" ([First Round Review: The Minimum Viable Testing Process](https://review.firstround.com/the-minimum-viable-testing-process-for-evaluating-startup-ideas/)). For Maven, the riskiest assumption was "will people pay ~10x more for a cohort-based course than a recorded one?" — tested with a single manually-run course that grossed $150K. For his food-delivery startup Sprig, the test was operational: a private chef, Craigslist drivers, and Catan pieces on a map to dispatch 40+ meals in one night. Two weeks, near-zero code.
4. **Concierge / manual versions.** Do the service entirely by hand for a handful of users. Graham's [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html): "You can sometimes get away with doing by hand things that you plan to automate later." Vanta (compliance automation) started as a spreadsheet before it was software.
5. **Design partners.** 3–5 companies that agree to use the rough product weekly and shape it. The commitment of their *time* is the evidence.
6. **Pre-sales / LOIs / deposits.** Money before product. An LOI (Letter of Intent — a written, usually non-binding statement that a customer intends to buy) is moderate evidence; a paid pilot is strong. LaunchDarkly's "first six-figure contract convinced skeptical VCs better than words" ([via Jackson](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea)).
7. **Retention and organic pull.** The strongest signal of all: people keep using it, and strangers arrive unbidden. Pinwheel got 133 **inbound leads** in 72 hours after announcing. ("Inbound leads" is sales jargon for potential customers who contact *you*, unprompted, rather than prospects your own outreach dug up. Inbound = they came to you; outbound = you went to them.) Flexport, post-pivot, "had trouble keeping up with the demand."

**[Evergreen]** Biyani's summary of the whole philosophy: "In the MVP strategy, you have no strategy: you throw things at a wall until it sticks. In the MVT world, you take your time to discover a strategy but once you have one, you move forward with conviction." Conviction is the *output* of testing, not the input.

## 6. Conviction vs. delusion

**[Evergreen]** From the outside, a founder with earned conviction and a deluded founder look identical. Both are certain. Both ignore skeptics. Both persist through rejection (Airbnb was rejected by essentially every investor; one prospect walked out mid-smoothie). The difference is invisible in their *confidence* and visible in their *evidence*:

| Dimension | Earned conviction | Delusion |
|---|---|---|
| Source of belief | Specific observed behavior (people paid, returned, referred) | Vision, analogy, "everyone will obviously want this" |
| Response to negative data | Updates the *plan* while holding the *mission* (pivots within the space) | Explains data away; blames marketing, timing, users |
| Skeptics | Can state the skeptics' case better than they can, and says exactly what evidence would prove the skeptics right | Dismisses skeptics as not getting it |
| Falsifiability | Has written kill criteria | "We just need more time" |
| What's held loosely | The current product | Nothing — idea and identity have merged |

**[Evergreen]** The Airbnb case is instructive precisely because it *looks* like delusion rewarded. It wasn't. By the time they persisted through mass investor rejection, the founders had direct evidence investors lacked. They had run real bookings, starting with their own apartment. And when they went door-to-door in New York photographing hosts' listings, they learned hosts were desperate for the recession-era income ([The Airbnbs](https://paulgraham.com/airbnbs.html), [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)). They also bounded their bet. Their goal, taped to the bathroom mirror, was "ramen profitability" — about **$4,000/month** ($3,500 rent + $500 food), the point where a startup covers its founders' bare living costs and no longer needs anyone's permission to keep going. Certainty about the mission, arithmetic about survival. That combination — not raw persistence — is the pattern.

**[Evergreen]** One more First Round-flavored test worth stealing: ask experienced founders not "would you buy this?" but **"would you start this company?"** The question forces them to weigh market, timing, and difficulty the way you must ([First Round Review on validation tactics](https://review.firstround.com/unconventional-tactics-for-validating-your-startup-idea/)).

## 7. Case studies: how four famous companies reached conviction

**Airbnb (2007–2009) — conviction from lived usage plus survival math. [Evergreen]** Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia couldn't make rent, aired mattresses during a sold-out design conference, and *were their own first hosts* — the organic-idea pattern in its purest form. Conviction built in layers: their own bookings → funding survival by selling novelty election cereal → YC, where Graham admits "we didn't even like the idea that much" but funded their energy → repeatedly traveling to New York to meet hosts in person. Graham remembers them showing up to YC dinners with rolly bags because "they'd always just flown back from somewhere" ([Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)). Those trips surfaced the recession-era "why now" (hosts needed money) and fixed the product (professional photos). The ramen-profitability target made persistence rational rather than reckless. ([The Airbnbs](https://paulgraham.com/airbnbs.html))

**Stripe (2010) — conviction from being the desperate user. [Evergreen]** Patrick and John Collison had run online businesses and felt payments pain directly. The insight was that *thousands* of developers shared it — but schlep blindness (banks! regulation! fraud!) kept everyone from acting ([Schlep Blindness](https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html)). Their empirical layer was extreme user-closeness: the famous "Collison installation" — when a founder said they'd try Stripe, the Collisons would take the person's laptop and integrate it on the spot ([Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)). Watching developer after developer go from skeptical to live-in-minutes was the conviction engine. It doubled as the product bar: seven lines of code where incumbents needed weeks.

**Figma (2012–2016) — conviction from a technology unlock plus a secret. [Evergreen] example, [2026]-relevant pattern.** Dylan Field and Evan Wallace's conviction came unusually early and analytically — but it was grounded in a demo, not a spreadsheet. Wallace's WebGL experiments — including his widely shared real-time [WebGL water simulation](https://madebyevan.com/webgl-water/), with raytraced reflections, refractions, and caustics running in an ordinary browser — *proved the unlock was real* before they committed ([Figma's founding story](https://www.indexventures.com/perspectives/figma-goes-public-thirteen-unforgettable-years-with-dylan-field/)). The secret — "design tooling will move to the browser and become multiplayer, like Google Docs" — was genuinely contrarian: professional designers were certain browser tools would be toys. Note the cost of a deep-tech why-now: four years of building before public launch in 2016. When your conviction rests on a technical unlock, the evidence you need first is *technical proof*, and your validation loop runs on demos to skeptical professionals rather than pre-sales.

**Cursor / Anysphere (2022– ) — conviction from a trend line. [2026]** Four MIT students (Michael Truell and co-founders) first spent, in Truell's words, "a year wandering the desert" on a *different* idea — AI autocomplete for CAD (computer-aided design software) and mechanical engineering — which failed the founder-market-fit test: they weren't the users. They pivoted to the domain where they *did* live in the future: coding. Their why-now was a bet on **scaling laws** — the empirical observation that model capability improves predictably as you add compute and data. Even with zero further breakthroughs, models would keep getting better. So an editor built AI-native — owning the whole surface rather than being a plugin like GitHub Copilot — would compound in value with every model release ([Contrary Research: Cursor](https://research.contrary.com/company/cursor); [Truell at YC AI Startup School](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ms-michael-truell-building-cursor-at-23-taking-on-github-copilot-and-advice-to-engineering-students)). Notice the structure: their conviction was a *falsifiable public bet* (scaling continues) plus a *contrarian product position* (fork the editor; don't build a plugin), validated the evergreen way — "dogfooding" daily (using your own product as a real user, i.e. "eating your own dog food") and shipping to developers whose retention was the score. It also shows kill criteria working: they killed the CAD idea, kept the thesis ("AI transforms professional tools"), and re-aimed it.

## 8. The Conviction Ladder — a protocol for your candidate ideas

**[Evergreen]** Run each candidate direction up this ladder. Each rung has a cost, a pass condition, and an implied kill. Do not skip rungs upward (you'll build on sand). Do not linger on a rung after passing it (that's validation theater). Expect the full climb to take 2–4 months per serious idea; Jackson's guidance is at least "a month or two digging deeply" before concluding anything.

**Rung 0 — Write the hypothesis. (1 day, $0.)** One page, Hale-style: the problem (who has it; how popular/growing/urgent/expensive/mandatory/frequent it is), the solution, the insight (which unfair advantage), the why-now (which unlock, and why the window is open *now*), and your Thiel secret. Then write your kill criteria for rungs 1–4 *now*, before you're attached. Pass: you can name 10 specific people or companies who plausibly have their hair on fire. Kill: you can't.

**Rung 1 — Desk check. (2–3 days, $0.)** Tarpit search: who tried this and died, and why exactly? Venture-scale arithmetic: buyers × realistic price × plausible penetration — does the best case clear ~$100M ARR potential? **[2026]** AI check: if the model providers ship this natively, do customers cancel? Pass: no unexplained graveyard, the math clears, and the idea survives the wrapper test. Kill: your differentiation reduces to "we'll execute better."

**Rung 2 — 30 problem interviews. (2–4 weeks, ~$0.)** Past behavior only. You're hunting for people who have *already* hacked together a solution — spreadsheets, contractors, duct-taped scripts. Existing spend of money or time is the strongest problem evidence there is. Pass: a tight segment where at least 1 in 3 describe the problem as a top-3 priority they're already paying something to address. Kill: universal politeness. (Interview technique: `08-talking-to-users.md`.)

**Rung 3 — Minimum Viable Test. (1–3 weeks, <$1k.)** Biyani-style: isolate the *riskiest* assumption only and test it with maximum hackiness — a landing page with a price on it, a manually-delivered service for 5 users, a Figma-style demo shown to 20 skeptical professionals. Force a real commitment: money, or scheduled recurring time. Worked example: waitlist page → 1,000 targeted visitors (≈$500 of ads or a well-placed post) → pass at ≥8–10% signup *plus* unsolicited follow-ups. Or B2B: 20 demo calls → pass at ≥5 agreeing to a weekly design-partner slot.

**Rung 4 — Sell it before it exists (B2B) or ship an embarrassing v1 (consumer). (4–8 weeks.)** B2B pass: 3+ signed design partners, of which at least 1 pays real money — even $500/month. The point is the pain of getting a purchase order approved, not the amount. Consumer pass: of your first ~100 users, a meaningful group returns weekly without prompting and some invite friends. Do everything manually behind the curtain — recruit users by hand, one at a time, per [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html): "your first users should feel that signing up with you was one of the best choices they made."

**Rung 5 — Pull. (ongoing.)** The top of the ladder isn't a number you hit. It's a change in the direction of force: inbound you didn't generate, users angry when it breaks, demand you struggle to serve. ("It was very, very obvious at Flexport that we had product-market fit as soon as we moved into freight" — [via Jackson](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/validating-your-startup-idea).) At this point conviction stops being a belief you maintain and becomes a fact you manage. This is also the point where fundraising conversations change character — see `05-fundraising.md` and `07-pitching.md`.

**[Evergreen]** Two meta-rules. First, run 2–3 candidates through rungs 0–2 *in parallel* — comparison is the cheapest cure for infatuation. Second, when an idea dies on a rung, harvest it: the interviews from a dead idea are the idea generator for the next one. Most pivots come from something users said while you were validating the wrong thing.
