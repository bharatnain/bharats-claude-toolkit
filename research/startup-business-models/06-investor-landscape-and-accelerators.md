# The Investor Landscape & Accelerators — YC, Techstars, and who writes checks

**Why this matters to you:** You're pre-idea in NYC. The moment you have something worth funding, you'll discover that "raising money" is not one market. It's a dozen overlapping ones, each with different players, terms, and unwritten rules. Your friends who succeeded knew which doors to knock on, in what order, and what each door costs in equity and time. This document maps the players: accelerators (YC — Y Combinator — and everyone else), the NYC seed ecosystem you're physically inside, the SF ecosystem you're weighing, and the full range of check-writers from angels to mega-funds. Read this alongside `05-fundraising.md` (the mechanics of raising) and `07-pitching.md` (how to actually pitch). This doc is about *who*; those are about *how*.

## Part 1: The taxonomy of check-writers

**[Evergreen]** Before naming names, learn the categories. Every early-stage investor falls roughly into one of these buckets. Each bucket has different incentives, which means different behavior toward you.

First, some vocabulary you'll see constantly:

- **The stage ladder — pre-seed, seed, Series A/B/C:** startup fundraising happens in named rounds of increasing size. **Pre-seed** is the first outside money (typically $100k–$1M). You raise it on little more than a team and a prototype, often from angels and specialist pre-seed funds. **Seed** (typically $1M–$5M) funds you to find real customers and early revenue. **Series A** (typically $8M–$20M+) is the first big institutional round. You raise it once you have evidence the business works. It's usually a **priced round** (a round where the company gets a formal valuation) with a board seat, and it's the milestone everyone measures seed-stage companies against — hence phrases like "Series A close rate" or "will they lead your A." Series B, C, and so on follow as you scale. The names are conventions, not laws, and round sizes drift up over time. But every investor in this doc defines themselves by which rungs they invest at.
- **VC (venture capital):** professional investors who manage other people's money and buy minority stakes in startups, hoping a few of those startups return the whole fund.
- **GP (general partner):** the person at a VC firm who makes investment decisions. **LP (limited partner):** the institutions and rich individuals whose money the GP invests. GPs answer to LPs. This shapes everything they do.
- **Fund:** a fixed pool of money (e.g., "$160M Fund IV") that must be invested over a few years and returned with profit roughly 10 years later.
- **Check size:** how much a given investor typically puts into one company.
- **Lead investor:** the investor who sets the terms of a round and usually invests the most. Others "follow."
- **Thesis:** a firm's stated belief about where returns will come from ("we back networked platforms," "we only do enterprise software").
- **Dilution:** the percentage of your company you give up when you sell shares or promises of shares.
- **Cap table (capitalization table):** the master spreadsheet of who owns what percentage of your company — founders, employees, every investor. "Getting on your cap table" just means becoming an owner of a piece of your company.
- **Follow-on:** any money an investor puts in *after* their first check — that is, investing again in your later rounds. "Strong follow-on support" means they keep backing you (and help you raise from others) as you grow.
- **Deal flow:** the stream of investable startups an investor gets to see and choose from. Investors compete to see the best companies early. Deal flow is their raw material, which is why their reputation with founders matters so much to them.

### The buckets

**Angels** **[Evergreen]** — individuals investing their own money, typically $5k–$100k checks. They decide fast (sometimes in one meeting), have no partnership to convince, and answer to no LPs. What they want: to be early on something exciting, sometimes just to help. What they give: money, intros, and social proof. Weakness: small checks, and the quality of their help varies wildly.

**Operator angels** **[Evergreen]** — angels who are current or former startup operators (a Stripe early employee, a founder who sold their company). This is the most valuable flavor of angel for a first-time founder. They've *done the job* recently, and their name on your cap table signals to VCs that insiders believe in you. A canonical example of the archetype: **Gokul Rajaram**. He built AdSense at Google, ran ads at Facebook and product at Square and DoorDash, and has made ~290 angel investments at $25k–$1M checks, including Figma, Airtable, and Coinbase ([his NFX Signal profile](https://signal.nfx.com/investors/gokul-rajaram)). In 2026, many operator angels from AI companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, Scale alumni) are among the most sought-after early checks. **[2026]**

**Solo GPs** **[Evergreen — the category; its prominence is 2020s-era]** — one-person venture funds managing LP money, writing $100k–$1M+ checks. Examples of the archetype, from small to enormous: **Charles Hudson** of [Precursor Ventures](https://precursorvc.com/), an SF-based pre-seed specialist best known for an early check into The Athletic, with a portfolio of hundreds of pre-seed companies (he's also [listed among top enterprise seed investors active in NYC](https://signal.nfx.com/investor-lists/top-enterprise-seed-new-york-city-investors) on NFX's Signal). **Lachy Groom** ([LGF](https://lachygroom.com/)), an ex-Stripe operator whose solo funds are among the most active pre-seed check-writers in the US. And at the extreme end, **Elad Gil** — a perennial fixture at the top of the [Forbes Midas List](https://www.forbes.com/midas/) of venture's best-performing investors and one of the most prolific solo investors in Silicon Valley (Airbnb, Coinbase, Anduril). He now raises billion-dollar-scale solo vehicles, proof that the "solo" label no longer means small. **[2026 names]** Solo GPs decide fast like angels but write checks like small funds. They live and die by founder referrals, so they tend to be founder-friendly.

**Seed specialists** **[Evergreen]** — firms whose entire business is leading pre-seed/seed rounds ($500k–$4M checks). BoxGroup, Lerer Hippeau, Primary, and Work-Bench in NYC are examples (detailed in Part 5; their SF counterparts — Uncork, Pear, Haystack, and others — are in Part 6). Because seed is their *only* product, they must be good partners at that stage. Their reputation with founders is their deal flow. They will actually work with you.

**Multi-stage funds** **[Evergreen principle, 2026 dynamics]** — firms like a16z (Andreessen Horowitz), Sequoia, General Catalyst, and Thrive that invest out of billion-dollar funds at every stage, from seed through pre-IPO (an **IPO**, initial public offering, is when a company lists its shares on the stock market). They increasingly write seed checks too. The catch, which every experienced founder will warn you about: a seed specialist with a $50M fund *needs* your $2M seed to work; a $5B multi-stage fund is often just buying an option on your Series A. If a multi-stage fund seeds you and then *doesn't* lead your A, other investors notice and read it as a red flag. That risk is called **signaling risk**. **[Evergreen]** The counterargument: a multi-stage brand name opens doors, and in the 2026 AI market the big funds move extremely fast and pay high prices. **[2026]** See `05-fundraising.md` for how to weigh this in an actual round.

**Accelerators** — covered in depth below. They're investors too: they buy equity with a standardized deal plus a program.

### What each actually gives you

**[Evergreen]** A useful mental model: **money is a commodity; everything attached to it isn't.** Rank investors by what comes attached — hiring help, customer intros, follow-on fundraising muscle, honest advice, brand halo. A famous angel's $25k can be worth more than an unknown fund's $250k because of what their name unlocks. This is what people mean by "smart money."

## Part 2: Accelerators vs. incubators vs. studios

Three terms that get confused. They are different businesses. **[Evergreen]**

- **Accelerator:** a fixed-length program (usually ~3 months) for a *batch* (cohort) of existing teams, ending in a **Demo Day** — a pitch event in front of investors. You keep full ownership and control; the accelerator buys a small stake on standard terms. YC invented the modern form in 2005.
- **Incubator:** a looser, often open-ended arrangement providing space, resources, and sometimes capital while you *find* the idea. Less batch pressure, more gestation. (AI2 Incubator, covered below, is a true incubator: you can join pre-idea.)
- **Venture studio (startup studio):** the studio *originates* the idea, hires founders to run it, and keeps a large stake — often 30–80%. Studios can be great for people who want a lower-risk path, but the equity math means you're closer to an early employee than a founder. If you want a "big game-changing startup" that is yours, a studio is generally the wrong vehicle. **[Evergreen]**

## Part 3: Y Combinator, in depth

YC is the accelerator against which all others are measured. Understanding it teaches you half the vocabulary of early-stage investing.

### The current deal **[2026]**

Verified from [YC's official deal page](https://www.ycombinator.com/deal): YC invests **$500,000** in every accepted company, structured as two SAFEs:

1. **$125,000 on a post-money SAFE for 7% of the company.**
2. **$375,000 on an uncapped SAFE with an MFN provision.**

Translation of every term:

- A **SAFE** (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is a contract where the investor pays now and receives shares later, when you raise a **priced round** (a round where the company gets a formal valuation). YC invented the SAFE in 2013; it's now the default early-stage instrument. See `05-fundraising.md` for full SAFE mechanics.
- **Post-money** means the ownership percentage is calculated *after* the investment is included. $125k for 7% implies YC is valuing you at $125,000 ÷ 0.07 ≈ **$1.79M post-money**. That fixed 7% is the real price of YC, and it's the same whether you're two people with a code repository or a company with $1M revenue.
- **Uncapped** means the second SAFE has no valuation ceiling. It converts at whatever valuation your next round sets.
- **MFN (Most Favored Nation)** means that if you later issue a SAFE on better terms to someone else, YC's uncapped SAFE automatically adopts those better terms. Per YC, it "will automatically convert in the priced round on the terms of the lowest cap Safe" ([YC deal page](https://www.ycombinator.com/deal)).
- YC also gets **pro rata rights**: the right to invest in your future rounds to maintain its ownership percentage ([YC deal page](https://www.ycombinator.com/deal)).

**Worked example [2026 numbers, Evergreen math]:** Say you do YC, then raise a $3M seed at a $25M post-money valuation.

- The $125k SAFE: already fixed at 7%.
- The $375k MFN SAFE: converts at $25M post-money → $375,000 ÷ $25,000,000 = **1.5%** additional.
- YC's total: ~8.5%. New seed investors: $3M ÷ $25M = 12%.
- Rough founder dilution so far: ~7% + 1.5% + 12% ≈ 20.5%, before an option pool (shares set aside for future employees, typically another ~10%; see `05-fundraising.md` for cumulative dilution math across rounds).

The higher your next-round valuation, the less the $375k costs you. YC deliberately built the deal so most of its money gets cheaper as you succeed.

Other terms worth knowing: the investment isn't contingent on hitting milestones, is committed at acceptance, and requires incorporation in the US, Canada, Cayman Islands, or Singapore ([YC deal page](https://www.ycombinator.com/deal)).

### Batch mechanics **[2026]**

YC historically ran two batches per year. It now runs **four** — Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall — each with roughly 150–200 companies ([Round Funded's 2026 batch guide](https://www.roundfunded.com/en/blogs/yc-batches-2026-dates-acceptance-rate); [ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/yc-acceptance-rate-2026-how-many-apply-how-many-get-in-and-what-it-takes) reports 2026 cohorts of ~199 and ~193 companies). The batch runs ~3 months in San Francisco. In-person attendance is expected in the current era — YC returned to in-person after its remote COVID years. The program is built around weekly group office hours with partners, talks from famous founders, a relentless focus on "launch and grow something every week," and Demo Day, where you pitch to an enormous audience of investors.

### Acceptance rate and what YC selects for

**[2026]** Acceptance is roughly **1%, ranging ~0.6–2% depending on the batch**. Summer 2025 was reported at ~0.6%, with application volume in the tens of thousands per cycle ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/yc-acceptance-rate-2026-how-many-apply-how-many-get-in-and-what-it-takes); [Round Funded](https://www.roundfunded.com/en/blogs/yc-batches-2026-dates-acceptance-rate)). Treat exact figures as estimates — YC doesn't publish official numbers — but "harder to get into than any university" is directionally right.

**[Evergreen]** What they select for, per YC partner Dalton Caldwell's canonical talk ["How to Apply and Succeed at YC"](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/It-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-yc): **clarity, self-awareness, and proof the team can build the product.** Caldwell's #1 application tip is to make it unambiguous that at least one founder can build the thing. He looks for "conciseness, clarity, and bluntness." Formidable, fast-moving teams beat polished ideas — YC assumes the idea will change.

### Application and interview advice

**[Evergreen]** — distilled from [Caldwell's talk](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/It-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-yc) and YC's own materials:

- Write the application like an engineer, not a marketer. Short, direct answers. No buzzwords. If a YC partner can't understand what you do in one sentence, you've failed the first filter.
- Show evidence of velocity: what have you built, launched, learned *recently*? A demo link beats adjectives.
- Caldwell explicitly notes the application is valuable even if rejected — it forces you to articulate how you differ from competitors. And applying at all matters: avoiding rejection "reduces the surface area to get lucky."
- The interview is **10 minutes**, rapid-fire, with partners interrupting. It is not adversarial. It's a test of whether you deeply know your users, numbers, and market. Practice answering in one or two sentences. Know your numbers cold. Don't pitch — converse.
- Rejection is weak signal. YC itself says many successful companies got in on a second or third application. Reapplying with visible progress is a *positive* pattern.

### Is YC worth 7%? Both sides, honestly

**The case for [Evergreen, with 2026 numbers]:**

- At the earliest stage, $500k for ~7–8.5% is competitive with market pricing. You're also buying an alumni network of tens of thousands of founders, Demo Day access to 1,000+ investors, and materially better fundraising outcomes — one analysis cites a ~40% Series A close rate within 12 months of Demo Day for YC companies ([ValueAddVC, "What Does Y Combinator Actually Give You?"](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/what-does-y-combinator-actually-give-you-the-real-value-beyond-the-500k-check)).
- The brand is a permanent credential. "YC-backed" changes how investors, candidates, and even customers answer your emails. That's especially valuable for a first-time founder with no track record — which is you.
- The batch structure manufactures urgency and peer pressure that reliably compresses 12 months of progress into 3.

**The case against [Evergreen]:**

- If you already have revenue, a product, and a network, 7% of value *you already created* is expensive. As one analysis puts it, for established founders "that 7% represents a substantial chunk of the value you've already created" ([Charisol](https://charisol.io/is-y-combinator-worth-it-for-established-founders/)).
- Batches of ~200 companies mean partner attention is thin, and partners can't be experts in every niche. You may get generic advice ([Michael Babich, "Is Y Combinator worth it by numbers?"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/y-combinator-worth-numbers-michael-babich)).
- YC filters great founders; it doesn't manufacture them. "YC accelerates, it does not create" — founders who get the most out of it arrive with momentum ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/what-does-y-combinator-actually-give-you-the-real-value-beyond-the-500k-check)).
- It locks you onto the venture-scale path. If your idea might be a great $10M/year business rather than a $10B one, YC's model (and your investors' incentives) will push you away from that. See `02-business-models.md` on venture-scale vs. not.

**The honest synthesis for you specifically [2026]:** as a first-time, pre-idea, technical founder without an existing investor network, YC is close to the strongest expected-value trade available — *if* you get in and *if* you're committed to the venture path. The dilution argument mostly applies to founders with existing traction.

## Part 4: The rest of the accelerator landscape

### Techstars — the other big network, mid-turnaround

**Model [Evergreen]:** Techstars (founded 2006, Boulder) runs many small programs of ~10–12 companies each, based in specific cities or attached to corporate partners, rather than one giant batch. That was historically its differentiator: mentorship-dense, local, "give first" culture. **Techstars NYC** is one of its flagship city programs.

**Terms [2026]:** As of the fall 2025 cohort, Techstars invests **$220,000** in two parts. First, $20,000 for 5% equity via a post-money Convertible Equity Agreement, taken as **common stock** — the same ordinary class of shares that founders and employees hold. (VCs normally demand **preferred stock**, which carries extra rights like being paid back first if the company sells. An investor taking common matters to you because it means fewer special rights hanging over your company.) Second, **$200,000 on an uncapped MFN SAFE** — an explicit echo of YC's structure, and a $100k increase over its prior deal ([TechCrunch, Apr 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/18/techstars-increases-startup-funding-to-220000-mirroring-yc-structure/); [Techstars investment terms](https://www.techstars.com/newsroom/investment-terms)). Worked example from that coverage: if your next round is at **$20M pre-money**, the $200k SAFE converts to ~1% more, making Techstars' total ~6%. ("Pre-money" is the value everyone agrees the company is worth *before* the new cash goes in; pre-money + new money raised = post-money.)

**The 2024–2026 turbulence [2026]:** Techstars over-expanded, then retrenched. It laid off ~17% of staff and wound down its $80M J.P. Morgan-backed "AdvancingCities" programs by end of 2024, following an earlier 7% cut ([Yahoo/TechCrunch](https://www.yahoo.com/news/techstars-laying-off-17-ending-203134787.html); [Technical.ly on the DC program ending](https://technical.ly/professional-development/techstars-dc-accelerator-jp-morgan-ending/)). Co-founder David Cohen returned as CEO, called the expansion era the "awkward teenage years," moved headquarters to NYC, and is refocusing on fewer, better programs — including restarting the original Boulder accelerator in 2026 ([Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2025/06/07/techstars-founders-startup-boulder-accelerator/); [Techstars' own June 2026 update](https://www.techstars.com/blog/innovation-in-action/techstars-update-june-2026)).

**How it differs from YC [Evergreen]:** smaller cohorts, heavier formal mentorship, city-embedded (you could do Techstars NYC without relocating), and a brand that — candidly — carries less fundraising power than YC's. Alumni outcomes vary more from program to program; the strongest programs (NYC among them) are much better than the median. If you get into both, take YC. If Techstars NYC is your option, scrutinize that specific program's recent alumni and their follow-on raises.

### a16z speedrun — the aggressive newcomer **[2026]**

Andreessen Horowitz's accelerator, originally games-focused, now general tech. Per the [official speedrun FAQ](https://speedrun.a16z.com/faq): it invests **up to $1M — $500k for 10% upfront on a SAFE, plus $500k in your next round within 18 months** — plus $8M+ in cloud/AI credits, no board seat, and pro rata rights. The program is 12 weeks, **San Francisco only, in-person mandatory** (SR007 runs July 27–October 11, 2026). The stated acceptance rate is **below 0.4%**. Note the price: 10% for $500k upfront is *more* dilution per dollar than YC's 7% for $125k+375k. You're paying for the a16z machine and the guaranteed follow-on. Multi-founder technical teams are preferred; solo founders are accepted.

### South Park Commons (SPC) — for the pre-idea phase **[2026]**

SPC is technically a community plus a fund for people in the "-1 to 0" phase — *before* the idea — which matches exactly where you are. Its **Founder Fellowship** offers **$400k for 7% on a standard SAFE, plus a guaranteed $600k in your next external round**, plus ~$900k+ in credits ([SPC Fellowship announcement](https://www.southparkcommons.com/news/f26-founder-fellowship/); [SPC blog, Spring 2026](https://blog.southparkcommons.com/p/spc-founder-fellowship-spring-2026)). Crucially for you: the fellowship bootcamp can be done from SPC's offices in **San Francisco, New York City, or Bangalore**. SPC has a real NYC presence, and you can apply without an idea. If you don't get YC and are still in exploration mode, SPC is arguably the best-fit program on this list for your current stage.

### Neo — small, elite, mentor-driven **[2026]**

Founded by Ali Partovi, known for extreme selectivity and a strong technical-talent network. On February 19, 2026, Neo announced its accelerator's next form, **Neo Residency** ([TechCrunch, Feb 19, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/19/ali-partovis-neo-looks-to-upend-the-accelerator-model-with-low-dilution-terms/)): ~3 months at Neo's SF (Jackson Square) offices plus a 2-week Oregon bootcamp. Neo admits teams on a rolling basis per [its own announcement](https://neo.substack.com/p/neo-residency-a-new-era) ("We're admitting teams on a rolling basis, so don't wait"), with TechCrunch reporting the first residents gathering in summer 2026. Cohorts are tiny: only ~12–15 startups (plus 5–8 college-student teams on $40k no-strings grants), with mentors like Cognition's president and Notion's CTO. Terms per [Neo's announcement](https://neo.substack.com/p/neo-residency-a-new-era): **$750k on an uncapped SAFE**, $450k+ in compute credits, *and* **participation rights for Neo to buy up to 5% of your next equity round**. The uncapped SAFE itself is genuinely low-dilution. Partovi's example: a next round at a $15M valuation converts the SAFE to ~5%; at $100M, just 0.75% ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/19/ali-partovis-neo-looks-to-upend-the-accelerator-model-with-low-dilution-terms/)). But the participation right means Neo can still purchase a meaningful additional stake at your next round's price. So the real cost isn't "set entirely by your next round" — read the documents. The tiny cohort means real partner attention.

### HF0 — the monastery for repeat founders **[2026]**

A 12-week live-in residency in San Francisco ("Hacker Fellowship Zero") where founders live in one building and do nothing but build. Terms are reported as **up to $1M for 5%** (structures have varied; verify current terms when applying) ([HF0](https://www.hf0.com/); [Everything Startups profile](https://www.everythingstartups.com/vc-funds/hf0-residency)). It explicitly targets *repeat* and exceptionally accomplished founders. As a first-timer, this is likely a later-career option for you.

### Entrepreneurs Roundtable Accelerator (ERA) — the NYC workhorse **[2026]**

NYC's largest homegrown accelerator: a 4-month program, **$150,000 on a post-money SAFE**, a 500+ mentor network, explicitly built around NYC industry access (finance, media, real estate, commerce) ([ERA Winter 2025 announcement](https://www.eranyc.com/2025/01/13/nycs-era-entrepreneurs-roundtable-accelerator-announces-participants-winter-2025-program-companies-receive-150000-investments-post-money-safe/)). Honest read: ERA's brand doesn't travel like YC's. But it's a genuine on-ramp into NYC's mentor and customer network without relocating, and its terms are standard. Before choosing it over just raising a small angel round, compare its recent alumni outcomes against the dilution.

### AI2 Incubator (now AI House) — the AI-native incubator **[2026]**

Spun out of the Allen Institute for AI in Seattle; rebranded as **AI House** in 2026 ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/seattles-ai2-incubator-rebrands-as-ai-house-and-adds-key-investor-as-managing-director/)). A true incubator: rolling admissions, and you can join pre-idea. Terms per [its FAQ](https://www.ai2incubator.com/faqs): **up to $600k on a SAFE with a $10M cap** (a cap is a valuation ceiling — the SAFE converts as if the company were worth at most $10M, no matter how high your next round prices you). The check starts at $100k solo / $200k for a team and is topped up as you show traction. Add **7% in common stock** for incubation and **$1M+ in non-dilutive compute credits** ("non-dilutive" = value you receive without giving up any equity — the credits, unlike the SAFE money, cost you no ownership). The AI-research-adjacent talent network is the draw; the Seattle location is the constraint.

### Sequoia Arc — the boutique **[2026]**

Sequoia Capital's company-building program: tiny cohorts (~10 companies), **$1M upfront** (equity terms are not publicly standardized; third-party sources describe roughly seed-like ~10% terms — treat as unverified), taught directly by Sequoia partners like Roelof Botha and Jess Lee ([TechCrunch launch coverage](https://techcrunch.com/2022/03/20/sequoia-arc-outliers/); [Startup Intros, Arc Fall 2026](https://startupintros.com/resources/sequoia-arc-fall-2026)). Getting Sequoia as your first investor is an enormous signal — with the same multi-stage signaling-risk caveat from Part 1 if they don't follow on.

## Part 5: The NYC seed ecosystem — your home field

First, the macro. NYC is the firm #2 startup ecosystem in the US, and at the early stage the gap with SF is narrower than most people assume. One widely repeated stat — handle it with care: Carta data covering the year ending March 2023 counted **543 Manhattan startups raising seed/Series A vs. 486 in San Francisco *city proper*** ([Propmodo, citing Carta](https://propmodo.com/exploring-the-link-between-office-market-dynamics-and-early-stage-startup-density/)). **[2023 data — often misquoted]** Two caveats. First, the comparison is Manhattan vs. one city, *not* the whole Bay Area — add the Peninsula, South Bay, and East Bay and the Bay likely comes out ahead. Second, it's now several years old. The defensible claim is "Manhattan's early-stage density rivals San Francisco's," not "NYC beats the Bay." More current: NYC has ~35 AI unicorns (a **unicorn** is a private startup valued at $1B or more — the term was coined in 2013 when that was rare; it no longer is) that raised a combined $17B, and 71% of NYC's venture-backed AI startups build vertical applications — software aimed at one specific industry, like law or insurance ([SVB, "Empire State of AI"](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/); [Tech:NYC's quarterly dashboard](https://www.technyc.org/q-dashboard-1) tracks the ecosystem's stats). **[2026]** The firms below are the canonical NYC early-stage names; all are verified active as of 2025–2026.

- **[Union Square Ventures (USV)](https://www.usv.com/)** — the most intellectually famous NYC firm. Thesis-driven since 2003 (networks, protocols, climate; Twitter, Etsy, Coinbase). Small fund by design, still writes early checks ([Visible.vc's 2026 NYC VC list](https://visible.vc/blog/venture-capital-firms-in-nyc/)). They fund theses, not trends — read their blog posts before pitching.
- **[First Round Capital](https://firstround.com/)** — seed specialist (Uber, Notion, Roblox at seed) with a large NYC practice and the best founder-education content in venture (First Round Review). Famous for its post-investment support infrastructure.
- **[Primary Venture Partners](https://www.primary.vc/)** — NYC-only seed firm, arguably the most NYC-committed institution on this list. It publishes the go-to *NYC Seed Report* and runs big NYC summits. Brian Schechter of Primary on the ecosystem: "New York City is on the brink of becoming synonymous with commercial prosperity in AI" ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/)). Heavy operational support model.
- **[BoxGroup](https://www.boxgroup.com/)** — David Tisch and Nimi Katragadda's early-stage fund. Quietly in a huge fraction of good NYC seed rounds (and many SF ones). Known for fast, low-drama, founder-first checks, often co-investing rather than leading.
- **[Lerer Hippeau](https://www.lererhippeau.com/)** — "born and bred in New York" seed fund with consumer DNA (Warby Parker, Glossier, Allbirds), now investing broadly including AI ([Visible.vc](https://visible.vc/blog/venture-capital-firms-in-nyc/)). Big alumni community.
- **[Thrive Capital](https://thrivecap.com/)** — Josh Kushner's firm, NYC's multi-stage heavyweight (OpenAI, Stripe, Instagram). Not really a cold-pitch seed shop — Thrive concentrates huge conviction bets — but it anchors NYC's late-stage credibility ([Rho's NYC VC guide](https://www.rho.co/blog/vc-firms-in-new-york-ny)).
- **[FirstMark Capital](https://firstmark.com/)** — early-stage, community-building machine (Shopify, Pinterest, DraftKings). Runs large NYC event series (Data Driven NYC, etc.) that are themselves networking on-ramps for you ([Visible.vc](https://visible.vc/blog/venture-capital-firms-in-nyc/)).
- **[Work-Bench](https://www.work-bench.com/)** — NYC's enterprise-software seed specialist (founded 2012, Jonathan Lehr & Jessica Lin). It announced a **$160M Fund IV in May 2025** — up from a $100M Fund III — and leads seed rounds with **$2M–$4M checks** ([Work-Bench's Fund IV announcement](https://www.work-bench.com/post/announcing-work-bench-fund-iv-160m-to-back-seed-founders-with-bold-enterprise-visions); [Fund III announcement](https://www.work-bench.com/post/announcing-our-100m-work-bench-fund-3)). If you're selling to banks, insurers, or other big enterprises, they can hand you customers.
- **[Betaworks](https://www.betaworks.com/)** — part studio, part seed fund, running themed "Camps" (accelerator-style cohorts) currently focused on AI/agents ([Betaworks](https://www.betaworks.com/)). Good for weird, early, product-led AI ideas.

Per [its NFX Signal profile](https://signal.nfx.com/firms/company-ventures), it invests from pre-seed onward with checks of roughly **$200k–$2M (sweet spot ~$1M)**, across fintech (financial technology), proptech (real-estate technology), digital health, enterprise SaaS (software as a service — software sold as a subscription), and consumer — a generalist spread across NYC's home industries. It rarely appears on national "top firm" lists, which is the point: it's a relationship-driven, NYC-embedded fund. The practical way in is through the NYC community itself — campus events, portfolio-founder intros — rather than a cold deck.

**NYC angel culture [Evergreen pattern, 2026 flavor]:** NYC angels skew toward operators from fintech, adtech (advertising technology), commerce, and media — plus a fast-growing bench of AI-company operators as OpenAI and others expand their NYC offices ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/)). Names to know, so the archetypes aren't all Bay Area:

- **Fabrice Grinda** — serial marketplace founder (OLX) turned the most prolific angel in the city. His NYC-headquartered [FJ Labs](https://fjlabs.com/) has made **1,000+ investments with 30+ unicorns** (Alibaba, Flexport, Delivery Hero, Rappi), operating "angel investing at venture scale" with a hard focus on marketplaces and network-effect businesses.
- **Kevin Ryan** — the archetypal NYC operator-investor: built DoubleClick, then founded or co-founded Gilt, MongoDB, and Business Insider. He now builds and backs NYC companies (especially healthcare and fintech) through his incubator-fund [AlleyCorp](https://www.alleycorp.com/), where he is founder & CEO.
- **Joanne Wilson** ("[Gotham Gal](https://gothamgal.com/)") — longtime NYC-based angel with a large personal portfolio, known for backing women-led consumer, food, and media startups and for being an accessible first check for underestimated founders.
- **David Tisch** — technically now institutional via BoxGroup (Part 5 above), but he built his reputation angel-style: fast, small, founder-first checks across a decade of NYC seed rounds. He remains a model of how NYC angel money behaves.

There are also organized **angel groups** — associations of individuals who screen deals together. They are a bigger part of the culture in NYC than in SF. The two most active: **New York Angels** (the city's largest, $100k–$1.5M collective checks, VC-style diligence) and **37 Angels** (a women-led network writing $50k–$200k checks with a fast decision process) ([Visible.vc's 2026 NYC list](https://visible.vc/blog/venture-capital-firms-in-nyc/)). The scene is smaller and more relationship-dense than SF's: fewer parties, more intros through work. Nomic.ai's co-founder captured it: "The New York AI ecosystem is incredible. It's definitely smaller than the Bay, but that makes it tight knitted in a way that is almost lacking in the Bay" ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/)). Practical entry points: FirstMark's public event series, Betaworks events, Primary's summits, and university networks (Columbia, NYU, Cornell Tech). See `09-networks.md` for how to actually work these rooms.

## Part 6: SF vs. NYC for an AI founder — the honest 2026 tradeoff

**The case for SF [2026]:** The concentration is not hype. SF startups raised **$111.7B in the first nine months of 2025 — ~45% of all US venture capital** — and the Bay Area accounted for ~$122B of the $159B in US AI funding in 2025 ([Growth List data roundup](https://growthlist.co/san-francisco-startups/); [SVB](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/)). SF has the frontier-model labs (the companies building the most advanced AI models, like OpenAI and Anthropic), the densest AI research talent, most of the accelerators above (YC, speedrun, Neo, HF0 are SF-mandatory), and a culture where every coffee shop conversation is an AI startup conversation ([Mission Local on AI founders moving to SF](https://missionlocal.org/2023/10/ai-founders-head-west-to-sf-and-find-the-city-awash-in-connections-ideas-and-funding/)). If your startup *is the model* — research-heavy, infrastructure, frontier capability — the "you have to be in SF" crowd is mostly right: your co-founders, first hires, and lead investors are disproportionately there.

### The SF seed firms — the counterpart to Part 5 **[2026]**

Since you're open to raising in SF, here is the SF/Bay Area equivalent of the Part 5 firm-by-firm list. Two structural differences from NYC. First, the multi-stage giants (Sequoia, a16z, Benchmark, Greylock, Kleiner Perkins, Khosla) all write seed checks in SF too — with the Part 1 signaling-risk caveat. Second, the operator-angel bench is the deepest anywhere (the Elad Gil, Lachy Groom, and Gokul Rajaram examples from Part 1 are all Bay Area figures). The dedicated seed names:

- **[SV Angel](https://svangel.com/)** — Ron Conway's firm, the closest thing SF has to an institution of angel/seed investing. Seed and growth checks; OpenAI, Airbnb, DoorDash, Pinterest. Sam Altman: "I am reasonably confident OpenAI would have fallen apart without his help." The classic "everyone's first call" network node.
- **[Uncork Capital](https://uncorkcapital.com/)** — founded by Jeff Clavier in 2004 (as SoftTech VC), one of the original "micro-VC" seed funds (small funds writing small early checks, back when that was novel), now an explicitly AI-leaning seed specialist led day-to-day by Andy McLoughlin. Seeded SendGrid (2009) and LaunchDarkly. Its portfolio now includes Groq — though [per its own portfolio page](https://uncorkcapital.com/companies/) that's a recent position ("Partners since 2025"), not an early seed bet.
- **[Pear VC](https://pear.vc/)** — Pejman Nozad and Mar Hershenson; self-described "specialists in pre-seed and seed" with SF and Menlo Park offices. Seeded DoorDash, Gusto, Guardant Health. Also runs its own PearX accelerator program.
- **[Haystack](https://haystack.vc/)** — Semil Shah's early-stage firm; writes $1–3M initial checks and can lead. Early in DoorDash, Instacart, Figma, Carta.
- **[Initialized Capital](https://initialized.com/)** — seed firm co-founded by Garry Tan (who left to become YC's CEO) and Alexis Ohanian (who left to start Seven Seven Six). The firm remains active at seed; it led Coinbase's seed round ([Ellty's SF seed investor list](https://www.ellty.com/blog/san-francisco-seed-investors)).
- **[Susa Ventures](https://susaventures.com/)** — seed firm (Leo Polovets and partners); early investor in Robinhood and Flexport ([Ellty](https://www.ellty.com/blog/san-francisco-seed-investors)).
- **[Floodgate](https://floodgate.com/)** — Mike Maples Jr. and Ann Miura-Ko, pre-seed/seed pioneers; backed Lyft, Twitch, and TaskRabbit "before they were obvious" ([Ellty](https://www.ellty.com/blog/san-francisco-seed-investors)).
- **[Conviction](https://www.conviction.com/)** — Sarah Guo's AI-native firm, "purpose-built to serve AI-Native, 'Software 3.0' companies"; $1M–$25M checks, "often the first investor"; Mistral, Cognition, Harvey. If you're building AI, this is the most 2026-relevant seed name on the list.
- **[Precursor Ventures](https://precursorvc.com/)** — Charles Hudson's pre-seed firm (the Part 1 solo-GP example). SF-based, though he invests in NYC companies too.
- **[South Park Commons](https://www.southparkcommons.com/)** — the fund attached to the pre-idea community covered in Part 4; SF and NYC.

The same research rule as Part 5 applies: verify each firm's *recent* activity in your specific space before pitching (see Part 7). Broader directories: [Ellty's SF seed investor list](https://www.ellty.com/blog/san-francisco-seed-investors) and [Waveup's Bay Area top-20](https://waveup.com/blog/top-vc-firms-san-francisco-bay-area/). Treat listicle details as leads to verify, not gospel.

**The case for NYC [2026]:** If your startup *uses* models to attack an industry — finance, healthcare, law, media, real estate, commerce — NYC is arguably better. 71% of NYC AI startups are vertical applications. The customers (44 Fortune 500 headquarters) are in the same subway system. And Manhattan's early-stage company formation rivals San Francisco proper's (543 vs. 486 seed/Series A raisers in the year ending March 2023, per Carta — though the Bay Area as a whole is still bigger; see the caveats in Part 5) ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/startup-insights/vc-relations/empire-state-of-ai/)). Primary's framing — SF owns foundation models (the big general-purpose AI models that other products are built on), NYC is becoming "synonymous with commercial prosperity in AI" — is the cleanest one-line version of the split. NYC's diversified economy also means less boom-bust monoculture ([Governing, on how the AI boom is reshaping SF](https://www.governing.com/artificial-intelligence/how-the-ai-boom-is-changing-san-francisco)).

**The synthesis for you [2026]:** You don't have to choose today. The best-trodden 2026 path is a hybrid: build and sell from NYC, but do an SF program (YC, speedrun, SPC-SF) if accepted. Three months in SF as a batch founder buys most of the network benefit without permanent relocation. Decide permanently only when you know whether you're building *the model* (go west) or *the application* (stay home, visit often). Capital travels: every SF fund invests in NYC companies, and a great company in NYC will get funded. What SF gives that genuinely doesn't travel is serendipity density — random collisions with the exact right person. Weigh that against your NYC life, honestly, when the time comes.

## Part 7: Building your target investor list, and what "fit" means

### How to research **[Evergreen process, 2026 tools]**

1. **Start from portfolios, not directories.** Find 10–20 companies adjacent to yours (same buyer, same sales motion, one stage ahead — *not* direct competitors). Who seeded them? Those investors have demonstrated, recent conviction in your space. This beats any "Top VC" listicle.
2. **Use the free databases:** [NFX Signal](https://signal.nfx.com/) (free, filterable by stage/sector/geography — e.g., its NYC enterprise-seed list surfaces 900+ investors, with people like Charles Hudson of Precursor among featured profiles), [OpenVC](https://www.openvc.app/investor-lists/venture-capital-firms-investors-new-york-city), Crunchbase, and firms' own portfolio pages. Verify *recency* — an investor's 2021 activity tells you nothing about their 2026 appetite.
3. **Tier the list.** Tier A: ~10 investors with thesis-perfect fit who can lead. Tier B: ~20 plausible leads or large followers. Tier C: angels and small checks. Practice-pitch Tier C first (see `05-fundraising.md` on running a process, and `07-pitching.md`).
4. **Qualify each name on three axes:** (a) Do they invest at your stage and check size? (b) Have they invested in your space in the last ~18 months? (c) Do they have a conflict — a competitor in their portfolio? A conflicted investor will take the meeting anyway, for information. Don't hand it to them.
5. **Get warm paths.** A warm path is an introduction through someone the investor already knows and trusts. For each Tier A name, find that path: a founder they backed (best), a mutual operator, an accelerator partner. Founder references are the currency — investors trust their own founders' judgment most. Cold outreach works occasionally, but only when it's hyper-specific to their thesis. `09-networks.md` covers this.

### What investor-founder fit actually means **[Evergreen]**

Fit is not "they said yes." It's four concrete things:

1. **Stage fit:** a seed specialist for your seed; a multi-stage fund only with eyes open about signaling risk.
2. **Thesis fit:** they already believe something that makes your company inevitable. You want investors you don't have to educate about the market — only about you.
3. **Behavior fit:** how do they act when things go badly? This is discoverable: **reference-check your investors** by calling 2–3 founders they backed — including one whose company *failed*. Ask: were they helpful, absent, or destructive at the low point? Every good investor expects to be referenced. Hesitation to provide founder references is itself the answer.
4. **Ambition fit:** their fund math must match your outcome. A $3B fund needs multi-billion-dollar outcomes to matter; a $60M seed fund can celebrate a $300M exit. Misaligned ambition becomes boardroom pressure later — see `04-unit-economics.md` and `02-business-models.md` for the math behind why fund size dictates investor behavior.

The deepest version of fit: you will likely be tied to your lead investor longer than the average American marriage lasts. You cannot fire them. Choose someone you'd want on your worst day, not the biggest name on your best day. **[Evergreen]**
