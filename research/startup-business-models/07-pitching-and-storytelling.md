# Pitching & Storytelling — how to pitch, in writing and in the room

**Why this matters to you:** You will pitch hundreds of times before you ever pitch an investor — to potential cofounders, first hires, early customers, and yourself. The pitch is not a performance layered on top of your company. It is a compression test of your thinking, and investors treat it that way. Your friends who raised money were not better speakers than you. They knew the unwritten grammar of what a pitch is supposed to prove, in what order, and in how many seconds. This document gives you that grammar: the standard deck, the narrative arc, the 30-second and 2-minute versions, cold email and warm intro mechanics, and how all of this shifts for AI startups in 2026. Read it alongside `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md` (the process around the pitch) and `06-investors-and-accelerators.md` (who you're pitching to).

---

## 1. What investors are actually evaluating (it's not the slides)

Sequoia Capital — the venture capital firm behind Apple, Google, and Stripe ("venture capital," or VC, is the industry that invests in startups in exchange for ownership; a "VC" is also shorthand for an individual investor at such a firm) — says this explicitly at the top of its famous pitch template: "It wasn't really the slides we liked — it was their ideas, the clarity of their thinking, and the scope of their ambition" ([Sequoia, "Writing a Business Plan"](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/)). **[Evergreen]**

Kevin Hale is a former partner at Y Combinator (YC — the most famous startup accelerator, meaning a program that funds and coaches batches of very early startups; Hale no longer appears on [YC's current people page](https://www.ycombinator.com/people), but his pitch lecture remains the standard reference). He breaks down what happens in an investor's head during a first meeting into three questions ([Kevin Hale, "How to Pitch Your Startup"](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6q-how-to-pitch-your-startup), [full transcript](https://ytscribe.com/v/17XZGUX_9iM)): **[Evergreen]**

1. **Do I understand it?** If the investor can't repeat your idea back correctly, nothing else matters.
2. **Am I excited by it?** Hale's framing: a startup idea is "a hypothesis for why your company is gonna grow really quickly." A good investor hears your pitch and imagines the rare paths by which you become a billion-dollar company. Your job is to make that imagining easy.
3. **Do I like the team and want to work with them?** Rapport, energy, honesty.

Beneath those three questions, early-stage investors are pattern-matching on four signals — none of which appear on a slide: **[Evergreen]**

- **Team quality signals.** Do the founders have "founder-market fit" — a specific, earned reason why *they* should build *this*? Are they technical enough to build it themselves? Liz Wessel of First Round Capital (an early-stage VC firm) notes that "founder backgrounds" get their own section in the investment memos partners debate after you leave the room ([First Round Review, "What You Can Really Expect at a VC Partner Meeting"](https://review.firstround.com/heres-what-you-can-really-expect-when-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-at-a-vc-partner-meeting/)). "Founder-market fit" itself is standard venture shorthand rather than her phrase, but it is what that memo section is probing for.
- **Clarity of thought.** Hale's point that "concision shows competence": a crisp answer signals you've thought deeply; a rambling one signals you haven't. This is why great founders answer hard questions in two sentences.
- **Rate of learning.** How much did you learn per week since you started? Investors ask "what have you learned from users?" precisely to measure this. See `08-talking-to-users.md`.
- **Market pull evidence.** Any sign the market is dragging the product out of you: users who complain when it breaks, customers who pre-pay, waitlists that grew without marketing. Pull beats push in every pitch.

One data point on how little time you actually get. DocSend, a document-analytics company that publishes yearly studies of thousands of real pitch decks, found investors spend on average about **3 minutes 44 seconds** reading a pitch deck. How the deck arrives matters enormously: decks that came through a referral got ~4 minutes 18 seconds of attention and converted to meetings 40–50% of the time, while cold-submitted decks got ~2 minutes 31 seconds and converted 3–5% of the time ([DocSend research, summarized at PitchGrade](https://pitchgrade.com/blog/what-investors-read-pitch-deck-docsend-data); DocSend's own metrics page is behind a signup wall, so these numbers come from a secondary summary, and they are long-recirculated figures from earlier DocSend studies rather than fresh 2026 data). Within that time, investors linger longest on three slides: **team (~1:02), financials (~0:52), and traction (~0:49)** — "traction" being the standard word for evidence that people actually use and pay for your product. The specific figures are dated; the principle — "investors skim; front-load your best material" — is **[Evergreen]**.

## 2. The narrative arc: pitching as storytelling

The single most-shared essay on pitch narrative is Andy Raskin's ["The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen"](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0), an analysis of the sales deck used by Zuora (a subscription-billing software company). It's about selling to customers, but every good seed pitch follows the same five-beat arc: **[Evergreen]**

1. **Name a big, relevant change in the world.** Don't open with your product or even "the problem." Open with a shift that makes the status quo untenable. Zuora coined "the subscription economy." Raskin quotes screenwriter Robert McKee: "what attracts human attention is change." This is your **"why now"** — and it doubles as the answer to the investor's silent question, *"why hasn't this been built already?"*
2. **Show there will be winners and losers.** The change creates stakes. Companies that adapt thrive; ones that don't go extinct. This activates loss aversion — the fear of missing the shift.
3. **Tease the "promised land."** Paint the desirable future state, concretely, *before* mentioning your product. The promised land is a new world, not your feature list.
4. **Introduce your product's features as "magic gifts"** for reaching that promised land. Raskin's metaphor: "your prospect is Luke, and you're Obi-Wan, furnishing a lightsaber." The customer (or the market) is the hero; you are the guide.
5. **Present evidence** that the story is coming true: traction, customer results, a demo.

Compressed: **status quo → insight about a change → inevitable future → you are the vehicle → proof it's already happening.** Every strong seed pitch is a version of this arc, whether or not the founder knows it.

Oren Jacob — former chief technology officer of Pixar, then a founder — adds the delivery layer in a classic First Round Review piece. Structure the pitch like a screenplay with a beginning, middle, and end. Compress the core story to **20 minutes** so a one-hour meeting has room for questions. Rehearse until it sounds spontaneous: "Your pitch has to be infused with humanity. The best stand-up comedians practice their routine for a year." He also recommends practicing with someone who knows nothing about your business and interrupts with random questions ([First Round Review, "Storytelling Tips"](https://review.firstround.com/tell-stories-like-this-to-take-your-fundraising-pitch-from-mediocre-to-memorable/)). **[Evergreen]**

Finally, the psychology layer. Oren Klaff's book *Pitch Anything* is the standard text on pitch-room dynamics ([summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/pitch-anything-oren-klaff/)). Two ideas are worth stealing; one deserves caution: **[Evergreen]**

- **Frames.** Every meeting is a collision of perspectives, and the stronger "frame" — the unspoken assumption about who is evaluating whom — wins. If you have to *explain* why you deserve attention, you've already lost the frame. Practically: don't beg, don't over-justify, don't open with "thank you so much for your time."
- **Neediness kills deals.** Klaff: "validation-seeking behavior is the number one deal killer." The credible way to be non-needy is to actually have alternatives — other interested investors, a business that works without funding. This is why running a tight parallel fundraising process (see `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md`) *is* the pitch tactic.
- Caution: Klaff's "prize frame" — acting like *they* must win *you* — works only when backed by real leverage. Faking swagger with no traction reads as delusion. Use the underlying principle (reduce neediness), not the theatrics.

## 3. The canonical seed deck: slide by slide

"Seed" is the first substantial funding round (see `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md` for amounts and terms). The standard seed deck is 10–12 slides. Here is the merged Sequoia/YC skeleton, with what each slide must *prove*.

Sequoia's template ([Writing a Business Plan](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/)) prescribes: **Company purpose → Problem → Solution → Why now → Market potential → Competition/alternatives → Business model → Team → Financials → Vision (what you'll have built in five years).** **[Evergreen]**

YC's version (per Hale and Michael Seibel) compresses further and reorders for demo-day-style speed: **What we do → Problem → Solution → Traction → Market → Team → Ask.** The key YC addition is the **insight**: what do you understand that others don't, and why does that translate into fast growth? **[Evergreen]**

Per-slide guidance, synthesized:

| Slide | Must prove | Notes from the legends |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Purpose / one-liner | You can be understood in one sentence | Sequoia: "a single declarative sentence." Hale's noun rule: name the *thing you make*, the *problem*, and the *customer*. |
| 2. Problem | The pain is severe, frequent, and felt by many | Tell it through one real user's story, with numbers ("Maria spends 6 hrs/week reconciling invoices by hand"). |
| 3. Solution | Your fix is 10x, not 10% | Start from the problem, not the technology (Hale). A screenshot or 30-second demo beats prose. |
| 4. Why now | A recent change makes this newly possible/urgent | This is Raskin's "name the change." If you can't answer "why hasn't Google done this," you don't have a why-now. |
| 5. Market | The prize is big enough for venture returns | Bottom-up math only (see §7). |
| 6. Product | It exists and works | Demo > screenshots > mockups > words, in that order. |
| 7. Traction | The market is pulling | Growth *rate* beats absolute numbers at seed. A chart up-and-to-the-right with real axes. |
| 8. Competition | You know the landscape and have an edge | Never "no competition" (see §7). Name alternatives incl. spreadsheets/status quo. |
| 9. Team | You are the ones who will win | Founder-market fit stories, not logos-and-titles résumés. |
| 10. Ask | You know what you're raising and what it buys | "$2M to reach $1M ARR in 18 months" — amount, milestones, runway. |

(Three terms you'll see constantly: **ARR** = annual recurring revenue, the annualized value of subscription income. **Burn** = the net cash your company spends per month. **Runway** = months of cash left at your current burn.)

## 4. The three lengths: 30 seconds, 2 minutes, full deck

You need the same story at three zoom levels, and you should write all three down. **[Evergreen]**

**The 30-second version** (elevator, intro email, first sentence of any meeting) is three sentences, per Hale:
1. What you do, in words a stranger's parent would understand. Good: "Airbnb is the first online marketplace that lets travelers book rooms with locals instead of hotels." Bad: "We transform the relationship between individuals and information."
2. The size of the pain or market ("40 million US freelancers waste a day a month on invoicing").
3. Your traction or unfair advantage ("we launched 8 weeks ago and are growing 30% month over month").

Hale's "X for Y" shortcut ("LinkedIn for the next billion internet users") works only when X is a household-name billion-dollar company and Y is obviously a huge market that plausibly wants X's model. "Buffer for Snapchat" fails both tests.

**The 2-minute version** adds the insight and the ask: what you do → who has the problem and how badly → your solution and the demo moment → *what you understand that others don't* → traction → team one-liner → what you're raising. This is what you use in a first conversation with an investor before opening any deck.

**The full deck plus 20-minute walkthrough** is §3, delivered per Oren Jacob: story compressed to 20 minutes, the rest of the hour for questions, never reading slides.

## 5. How YC's application forces clarity — and demo day compression

Even if you never apply to YC, [its application questions](https://www.ycombinator.com/howtoapply.html) are the best free pitch-editing tool in existence, because each question is an X-ray for a specific weakness: **[Evergreen]**

- **"Describe what your company does in 50 characters or less."** Kills jargon instantly. Fifty characters is roughly "Stripe: payment APIs for developers."
- **"What do you understand about your business that other companies in it just don't get?"** This is *the* insight question. Alumni guides consistently say three things about it: it's the most memorable answer in a strong application; you can only answer it well if you've actually talked to customers; and the best answers are counterintuitive and backed by data ([YC application guides](https://www.flowjam.com/blog/yc-application-tips-2025), [example answers](https://shizune.co/yc-application-examples/who-are-your-competitors)). If a smart outsider could guess your answer in ten minutes, it isn't an insight yet — go back to `01-idea-evaluation-and-conviction.md`.
- **"Who are your competitors?"** Claiming none signals you haven't looked. Name them and beat them on the page.

**Demo day pitches** — the one-minute investor presentations at the end of an accelerator batch — are the extreme compression case: ~60 seconds, ~150 words. The formula, from analyses of YC demo day pitches ([guide](https://zyner.io/blog/yc-demo-day), [8-line breakdown](https://newsletter.startupistanbul.com/p/the-8-line-pitch-what-i-learned-from)): open with the plain-words description ("We build a database for hospitals" — no "decentralized," no "next-generation," because every second an investor spends decoding your description is a second they miss your growth numbers), then problem → solution → **traction with your strongest number unmissable** → team credibility → ask. If you're pre-revenue (no paying customers yet), show "proxy traction": signed letters of intent (an **LOI** is a written but non-binding statement from a customer that they intend to buy once you can deliver), a waitlist, or the sheer speed at which you shipped. **[Evergreen]** in structure; the one-minute format itself is a **[2026]**-era YC convention.

The meta-lesson: if your pitch works at 60 seconds, the 20-minute version will work. The reverse is not true.

## 6. Handling Q&A and objections

From Liz Wessel's First Round piece on partner meetings ([source](https://review.firstround.com/heres-what-you-can-really-expect-when-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-at-a-vc-partner-meeting/)): **[Evergreen]**

- **Expect constant interruption — and welcome it.** Questions mid-slide, even off-topic ones, are engagement, not rudeness. Prepare a short-slide version for interruption-heavy rooms and a longer one for quiet rooms.
- **Answer the question behind the question.** "How will you get customers?" often really means "is your CAC (customer acquisition cost — what you spend to win one customer) going to eat your margins?" Clarify before answering: "Are you asking about channel strategy or acquisition economics?"
- **Practice altitude switching.** You must move between five-year vision and this-week detail without gears grinding.
- Partner meetings — the full-firm pitch, usually the last step before a decision — run ~60 minutes with 5–15 investors. Firms Wessel surveyed issue term sheets to 25–60% of companies that reach that stage, and usually decide within 24 hours. (A **term sheet** is the investor's written offer to do the deal — how much they'll invest, at what valuation, on what terms. Getting one is the "yes" moment of a fundraise.) These numbers are from the First Round article, published August 2024, not fresh 2026 data; the structure is **[Evergreen]**.

On objections specifically: **[Evergreen]**

- **Never bluff.** "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" beats a confident wrong answer. Remember: they're scoring rate of learning and honesty, not omniscience.
- **Pre-empt the two worst objections yourself.** Every company has a "hard question" — **churn** (the standard term for customers cancelling or leaving), regulation, incumbent response. Raising it before they do converts a weakness into a clarity signal.
- **Objections are rarely requests for information; they're tests of how you think under pressure.** Two crisp sentences, then stop talking. Founders lose deals by answering a 10-second question for four minutes.

## 7. The mistakes legends call out by name

- **Jargon and buzzwords.** Hale: ambiguity, complexity, and marketing-speak trigger "ad blindness" — investors literally stop parsing. Use language you'd use with your mom. **[Evergreen]**
- **Top-down TAM slides.** TAM/SAM/SOM = total / serviceable / obtainable addressable market — jargon for "how big is the prize." The lazy version — "industry report says $250B; we capture 1% = $2.5B" — is a distrust signal because it proves nothing about your ability to acquire a single customer ([pitch-mistake analyses](https://investorreadycapital.com/news-insights/pitch-deck-mistakes-that-stop-fundraising), [Viktori](https://viktori.co/10-pitch-deck-mistakes/)). Do it **bottom-up**: *"US dental practices: ~130,000. Our wedge: the ~30,000 multi-dentist practices. Price: $500/month = $6,000/year. Obtainable market: 30,000 × $6,000 = $180M ARR — and expansion into the other 100,000 practices and adjacent workflows takes it past $1B."* (A **wedge** is startup jargon for the narrow initial segment you enter first — here, multi-dentist practices — before expanding to the broader market.) That one calculation proves you know who buys, at what price, and how many exist. **[Evergreen]**
- **"We have no competition."** Reads as either "I didn't look" or "there's no market." The status quo (spreadsheets, interns, doing nothing) is always a competitor. **[Evergreen]**
- **Hockey-stick fantasy projections.** A "hockey stick" is a revenue chart that runs flat, then suddenly turns steeply upward — like the blade and shaft of a hockey stick. A curve from $50K to $5M ARR with no stated drivers destroys credibility. Instead, derive it: "we add 50 customers/month at 95% monthly retention today; this raise funds 3 sales hires, each historically ramping to 25 deals/month, so 125/month by month 12." Defensible assumptions beat impressive numbers. **[Evergreen]**
- **Walls of text and 25-slide decks.** DocSend's data (§1): investor attention is a fixed few minutes no matter how many slides you send, so a 25-slide deck just spreads that scarce attention thinner. Twelve slides, big fonts, one idea per slide.
- **Reading the slides.** Jacob: know the material well enough to pitch with the projector off.
- **Preamble.** Hale: no wind-up, no agenda-slide theater, no company-history saga. First sentence = what you do.

## 8. Cold email that gets meetings

Michael Seibel — cofounder of Twitch and the longtime YC partner who ran YC's accelerator for years — has the canonical guide ([How to Email Early Stage Investors](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-email-early-stage-investors), [YC video version](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-cold-email-investors-michael-seibel/)). (He stepped down from his YC leadership role in 2024 and transitioned to Partner Emeritus in March 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/michael-seibel-is-leaving-y-combinator/)), but this guide remains the standard one.) His structure is three sentences, readable in under 60 seconds: **[Evergreen]**

1. **What you do, zero jargon.** Seibel: he's often distracted — "optimize for that."
2. **Why this investor should care.** Pick only your strongest cards: huge market, launched product, real growth, notable/technical founders, or a controversial insight about the problem.
3. **The specific ask.** Advice, investment, or an intro — but *don't ask for a call or meeting*. Seibel: "your goal here should just be to get a conversation going." Let the investor escalate.

Mechanics: send from a company email address with your real name. Walls of text get slow or no replies. Skip flattery. And — notably — Seibel says just email directly rather than spending weeks engineering a warm intro if you don't have one. Attach a deck only in a standard format (PDF).

A realistic example in his format:

> Subject: Cursor-style autocomplete for radiologists
>
> Hi Sarah — I'm Bharat, cofounder of Contrast. We make software that drafts radiology reports as the doctor reads the scan, cutting reporting time ~40%. We launched 10 weeks ago; 4 imaging centers pay us $2K/month each and usage is growing 25% month over month. My cofounder spent 6 years building ML at [known company]; I was a radiology resident. We're raising a $2M seed — could I send over our deck and one-pager?

Four sentences, three nouns (product, problem-owner, customer), one number-backed traction claim, one credible-team line, one soft ask.

**The funnel math, so you budget effort correctly** — **[2026]** numbers, **[Evergreen]** lesson: cold email reply rates to investors run roughly 1–5%, versus ~34–58% response on warm paths. One dataset puts funding conversion at ~8% for cold-originated conversations versus far higher for referred ones ([OpenVC](https://www.openvc.app/blog/how-to-cold-email-vc), [comparison data](https://growleads.io/blog/warm-outreach-vs-cold-email/), [Metal](https://www.metal.so/collections/warm-intros-vs-cold-email-2025-conversion-data-referral-playbook)). Concretely: 200 well-targeted cold emails ≈ 2–10 conversations; 50 warm intros ≈ 25–30 conversations. Cold email works — it's just a 10x-worse-yield channel, so it's the supplement, not the strategy.

## 9. Warm intros: why they dominate, and the follow-up cadence

A **warm intro** is an introduction to an investor from someone they already trust — a founder they've backed, a co-investor, an operator they respect. A Harvard Business School survey of ~900 institutional VCs found roughly **58% of deals originate through networks and referrals versus ~10% from unsolicited cold inbound** ([summarized at Gasimo](https://gasimo.org/warm-intro-vs-cold-outreach-what-the-data-actually-says/), [SheetVenture](https://sheetventure.com/fundraising-knowledge/what-makes-certain-intro-paths-more-valuable-than-direct-cold-email)). **[Evergreen]** dynamic; specific percentages **[2026]**.

Why: the introducer's reputation is collateral. A portfolio founder — a founder whose company the investor already funded — saying "you should meet Bharat" transfers trust you haven't earned yet. VCs spend a huge share of their week maintaining exactly this referral network.

**Mechanics** **[Evergreen]**:
- Best introducers, in rough order: founders the investor backed > co-investors/angels (**angel investors** are individuals who invest their own money in very early startups, versus VCs who invest a fund's money) > respected operators > lawyers/accountants (weak).
- Always use the **double opt-in forwardable email**. You send your introducer a short, self-contained blurb — essentially your Seibel three-sentence email. They forward it, asking the investor if they want the intro. This lets the investor say yes before meeting you and gives your champion zero work.
- **Getting intros when you know nobody** (Bharat's actual situation): the path is founders, not investors. Cold-email *founders* one or two stages ahead of you asking for advice, not intros. Genuinely learn from them. Intros follow naturally once they believe in you. See `09-networks-and-social-capital.md`.

**Follow-up cadence** **[Evergreen]**:
- Same day after a meeting: short thank-you with the 2–3 things they asked for (metrics, deck, customer references).
- No response after a pitch: follow up at ~1 week, then ~2 weeks, each time with **new information** ("closed two more customers since we spoke," "hit $30K MRR" — **MRR** = monthly recurring revenue). Updates with momentum are follow-ups that don't smell needy (Klaff's rule again).
- Silence after three touches is a no. Move on, but add them to your monthly investor-update list, because this round's pass is next round's pre-sold lead. First Round's fundraising-wisdom compilation stresses running the process in a compressed window precisely so partners hit "the sweet spot between FOMO and disinterest" (**FOMO** = fear of missing out) and race each other ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-fundraising-wisdom-that-helped-our-founders-raise-18b-in-follow-on-capital/)). The calendar is a storytelling device too.

## 10. Pitching in 2026: how AI-startup pitches differ

Everything above still applies, but AI-era investors have developed specific new reflexes. All of this section is **[2026]**.

- **Demo-first: "show the magic in 60 seconds."** Investors have seen a thousand "AI-powered X" decks, so a live demo of the wow moment now leads the meeting instead of ending it. Top-funded AI decks embed live links and QR codes so investors *experience* the product rather than imagine it ([AI Funding Tracker analysis](https://aifundingtracker.com/best-ai-startup-pitch-decks/)). But the counter-reflex is equally strong: VCs are burned by "thin GPT wrappers" — products that just repackage someone else's AI model behind a simple interface — that demoed well but lost users once the AI proved unreliable ([CRV, "What Investors Look For in 2026"](https://www.crv.com/content/ai-startup-funding)). So pair the demo with reliability evidence: task success rates, eval results (an **eval** — AI-industry shorthand for "evaluation" — is a systematic test that measures how accurately your AI performs its task across many examples, rather than a few cherry-picked demos), retention.
- **"Doers, not talkers."** Sequoia's 2026 AI Ascent framing: pitch systems that *complete multi-step work* with measurable outcomes, not assistants that answer questions. The problem slide shifts from "this workflow is inefficient" to "this work costs $X/year, recurs, and has a named budget owner." The traction slide shifts from "users tried it" to "paid pilots (trial deployments the customer pays for), retention, completed tasks, ROI" ([InnMind summary of Sequoia AI Ascent 2026](https://blog.innmind.com/sequoia-ai-ascent-2026-what-ai-founders-should-change-in-their-pitch-deck/)).
- **Defensibility gets its own interrogation.** "We use GPT/Claude" is not a **moat** (venture jargon, borrowed from castle moats: a durable competitive advantage that rivals can't easily copy), and prompting advantages erode in months. The answers investors accept: workflow depth (embedded in real business processes); proprietary data that improves the product with usage; integrations into systems of record (enterprise jargon for the authoritative databases a business runs on — the CRM, or customer relationship management system, holding its customer list; the accounting ledger; the ERP, or enterprise resource planning system, running its operations; whatever everything else must reconcile against); measured reliability with recovery logic; distribution advantages; and pricing tied to outcomes rather than seats ([InnMind](https://blog.innmind.com/sequoia-ai-ascent-2026-what-ai-founders-should-change-in-their-pitch-deck/), [Pitch Protocol](https://www.pitchprotocol.vc/blog/how-to-pitch-an-ai-startup-to-vcs-2026/)). See `10-ai-era-playbook.md`.
- **Margin questions you must be ready for.** Inference — the compute cost of running the AI model for each customer — is a real cost of goods sold, unlike traditional software, so **gross margin** (your revenue minus the direct cost of serving each customer, expressed as a percentage of revenue) becomes a live question. Expect: *What's your gross margin today? How does it improve as models get cheaper and you optimize?* Have a worked answer: "Each $500/month customer costs us ~$120/month in inference today — a 76% gross margin, since ($500 − $120) / $500 = 76%; caching plus routing easy cases to a small model cuts that to ~$60 by year-end (88%)." If your margin is 40% and your only plan is "models will get cheaper," expect a pass. See `04-unit-economics-and-money.md`.
- **Evidence of velocity.** Because AI compresses building time, shipping speed is itself a team signal: "two founders, 10 weeks, live product, 4 paying customers" now carries the weight that a year of stealth development (building in secret before launch) used to. Sequoia partners note strong AI startups reaching $1M+ revenue per employee — the bar for what a small team is expected to accomplish has moved.
- **The services reframe.** Sequoia's Julien Bek: "For every dollar spent on software, six are spent on services." The strongest 2026 market slides often size the *labor* being replaced, not the software category — a bottom-up calculation against salaries, not license fees.

## 11. Annotated example: a 10-slide seed deck

Fictional company: **Ledgerline — AI bookkeeping agent for restaurant groups.** (Illustrative only; numbers invented to show what *kind* of evidence each slide needs.)

1. **Title / one-liner** — "Ledgerline closes the books for restaurant groups automatically." *Must prove: you can be understood in one sentence.* Logo, one line, founders' emails. Nothing else.
2. **Problem** — "A 5-location restaurant group spends ~$4,000/month on bookkeeping and still closes its books 3 weeks late; 2 of our first 10 customers had six-figure theft they caught months late." *Must prove: severe, frequent, quantified pain with a budget owner — told through a real (anonymized) customer.*
3. **Why now** — "Frontier models crossed reliable invoice-and-POS (point of sale — the checkout/till system where a restaurant rings up orders) reconciliation accuracy in 2025; meanwhile the bookkeeper workforce is shrinking 4%/year." *Must prove: a recent change (Raskin's 'name the change') that explains why this is newly possible and why incumbents' architecture can't follow.*
4. **Solution + demo** — 60-second recorded demo: emailed invoice → categorized, reconciled against POS data, anomaly flagged, books closed. *Must prove: the magic works — and (2026) show a reliability number on-slide: "97.4% line-item accuracy across 41,000 transactions, human review for the rest."*
5. **Traction** — chart: $0 → $28K MRR in 6 months; 19 restaurant groups; 25% month-over-month growth; zero churned customers; 2 case-study quotes. *Must prove: pull. Growth rate and retention beat absolute size at seed.*
6. **Market (bottom-up)** — "~65,000 US multi-location restaurant groups × $18K/year average contract = $1.2B obtainable; bookkeeping labor for all US restaurants is a ~$9B/year spend (the services reframe)." *Must prove: a venture-scale prize built from unit math, not a "capture 1% of a Gartner number" slide (Gartner is a market-research firm whose industry-size estimates get lazily cited in decks).*
7. **Insight / competition** — 2×2 chart or table vs. QuickBooks + human bookkeepers, outsourced firms, horizontal AI-accounting startups. Insight line: "Everyone builds horizontal AI accounting; restaurant books are won at the POS integration — we've built the only agent reconciling against all top-5 restaurant POS systems, and every reconciliation makes our matching data better." *Must prove: you know the landscape, and you understand something others don't (the YC question) that compounds.*
8. **Business model & margins** — "$500–$2,500/month per group by location count; gross margin 81% today (inference ~$95/customer/month), path to 90% via model routing; CAC $1,900, paid back in 2.5 months." *Must prove: the unit economics — the per-customer profit-and-loss math — work, and you know your inference costs cold (the 2026 interrogation).*
9. **Team** — "CTO: 6 years payments-ML at Stripe. CEO: family ran 3 restaurants; did the books for a decade. Shipped v1 in 9 weeks." *Must prove: founder-market fit and velocity — a story, not a résumé.*
10. **Ask** — "Raising $2.5M seed → 20 months runway → milestones: $150K MRR, self-serve onboarding (customers can sign up and start without a salesperson), 2 more POS integrations — the Series A metrics." (The **Series A** is the next, larger funding round after seed — see `05-fundraising-mechanics-2026.md`.) *Must prove: you know what the money buys and what the next round requires.*

Appendix slides (shown only if asked): financial model, security/compliance, detailed eval methodology, full competitive teardown.

## 12. The compressed rulebook

1. First sentence = what you do, in your mom's vocabulary. **[Evergreen]**
2. Three nouns: the thing, the problem, the customer. **[Evergreen]**
3. Story arc: change in the world → winners and losers → promised land → your product as the vehicle → proof. **[Evergreen]**
4. Bottom-up market math only. **[Evergreen]**
5. Never "no competition"; never hockey sticks without drivers. **[Evergreen]**
6. Investors buy team, clarity, learning rate, and market pull — the deck is just the delivery mechanism. **[Evergreen]**
7. Warm intros ~10x cold email; get them through founders, not investors; cold email is the supplement and Seibel's three sentences are the format. **[Evergreen]** principle, **[2026]** conversion numbers.
8. Follow up only with new momentum. Neediness kills. **[Evergreen]**
9. In 2026 AI pitches: demo the magic in 60 seconds, prove reliability, know your inference margins, and pitch outcomes/workflows, not "AI-powered tools." **[2026]**
10. If the 60-second version doesn't work, the 20-minute version won't either. **[Evergreen]**
