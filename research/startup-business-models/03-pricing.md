# Pricing — how to actually determine what to charge

**Why this matters to you:** Pricing is the highest-leverage decision most first-time founders treat as an afterthought. A 1% improvement in price flows almost entirely to profit. A 1% improvement in customer acquisition or retention does not. Yet founders routinely spend a thousand hours on product and one hour on price. Worse, the default instinct of a technical founder — charge little, because the code cost you "nothing" to write — is exactly backwards. And it quietly determines what kind of company you can build: your price sets your sales model, the market size investors will fund, and even which customers take you seriously. This doc gives you the frameworks the pros use, the research methods that work before you have revenue, and a worksheet you can run on any idea in an afternoon. For how pricing plugs into margins and payback math, see `04-unit-economics.md`; for the business-model archetypes pricing lives inside, see `02-business-models.md`.

---

## 1. The three ways to price — and why only one is correct

There are exactly three families of pricing logic. **[Evergreen]**

**Cost-plus pricing:** figure out what the product costs you to deliver, then add a markup. This is how commodities, groceries, and contract manufacturing are priced. It is almost always wrong for software, because your marginal cost is near zero and has *no relationship* to what the product is worth to the buyer. Patrick McKenzie (known online as patio11, the most-cited writer on software pricing) diagnosed the underlying psychology two decades ago: developers price off their own costs, and "technical founders perceive code as being worth its cost and its cost to them is zero." As he put it in [You Can Probably Stand To Charge More](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2006/08/14/you-can-probably-stand-to-charge-more/), "the demand curve is external to you" — how long the thing took you to build is invisible and irrelevant to the customer. **[Evergreen]** (One 2026 caveat: AI products have real, non-trivial marginal costs — **inference**, the computing bill you pay each time the AI model runs to answer a request. So cost becomes a *floor* you must know, even though it's still not how you set the price. See section 9.)

**Competitor-based pricing:** find comparable products and price near them. Useful as a sanity check, and as an *anchor in the buyer's head* — they will compare you to something whether you like it or not. But it is dangerous as a strategy: you inherit your competitor's mistakes, and if you're doing something genuinely new there is no true comparable. **[Evergreen]**

**Value-based pricing:** estimate the economic value your product creates for a specific customer **segment** (a defined slice of customers, e.g. "mid-size e-commerce companies"), and charge a fraction of that value. This is the winner. Every serious pricing practitioner converges on it: Madhavan Ramanujam of Simon-Kucher (the world's largest pricing consultancy; author of *Monetizing Innovation*), Patrick Campbell (founder of ProfitWell, the pricing-analytics firm for SaaS companies — **SaaS** means "software as a service," software sold as a subscription rather than a one-time purchase; ProfitWell was later acquired by Paddle), McKenzie, and every pricing post from a **VC** (venture capitalist — an investor who funds startups) you'll read. **[Evergreen]**

The standard heuristic: quantify the value you create and capture roughly **10× less than you deliver** — the "10x rule" that shows up across enterprise sales lore ("enterprise" means selling to large corporations). If your tool saves a customer $500,000/year, a $50,000/year price is easy to justify in a procurement conversation. (**Procurement** = the corporate purchasing department at a large company — the people who approve and negotiate every vendor contract. You'll meet them in any deal over roughly $25K.) The customer keeps 90% of the surplus and still feels like a winner. The enterprise team at a16z (Andreessen Horowitz, one of the largest venture capital firms) makes the same point about AI-era deals: AI companies are "replacing labor budgets, whose size exceeds traditional software budgets, giving AI companies meaningfully larger contract sizes than their historical software counterparts" ([a16z, From Demos to Deals](https://a16z.com/insights-for-enterprise-ai-builders/)). **[Evergreen]** principle; the exact multiple you can capture varies by competition and how provably you can show the value.

**Worked example.** Suppose your product automates invoice reconciliation for mid-size e-commerce companies. A customer's finance team spends 2 full-time employees × $80,000 **fully-loaded** = $160,000/year on this. ("Fully-loaded" means the true cost of an employee to the company: salary *plus* benefits, payroll taxes, equipment, and overhead — typically 1.25–1.4× base salary. Always use the loaded number when computing the labor you replace, because that's what the buyer actually spends.) Your product eliminates ~70% of that work: $112,000/year of value. Value-based logic says charge $10,000–$25,000/year (capturing ~10–20%). Cost-plus logic ("our servers cost $30/customer/month, charge $99/month") would put you at $1,188/year — leaving 95% of the achievable price on the table *and* signaling to the buyer that this is a toy, not a finance system.

## 2. Price before product: researching willingness to pay at seed stage

("Seed stage" = the earliest phase of a startup, around its first outside funding, usually before meaningful revenue.)

The single most important idea in *Monetizing Innovation*: **have the willingness-to-pay conversation before you build, not after.** Ramanujam's research at Simon-Kucher found that 72% of new products miss their revenue targets, and that roughly 80% of companies never ask customers about price until the product is done ([First Round Review, "It's Price Before Product. Period."](https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/)). His four failure modes are worth memorizing, because you will be tempted into each: **[Evergreen]**

- **Feature shock** — cramming in features nobody will pay for. Amazon's Fire Phone had four cameras and facial recognition, and ended in a $170M write-down (a "write-down" = the company officially admitting on its books that inventory it built is now worth far less).
- **Minivation** — right product, price too low, revenue left on the table. Asus's €299 mini-notebook sold out with demand 9× supply — a screaming signal it was underpriced.
- **Hidden gem** — a valuable thing you never monetize because you're afraid of it.
- **Undead** — a product that should never have shipped, which honest price conversations would have killed early.

### 2a. How to ask about price in customer interviews

Do not ask "would you pay for this?" Everyone says yes to be polite — see `08-talking-to-users.md` for why hypothetical questions are poison. Instead, use Ramanujam's three questions, asked after you've walked a real prospect through the problem and your proposed solution: **[Evergreen]**

1. "What do you think would be an **acceptable** price for this?"
2. "What would be an **expensive** price — you'd think hard, but might still buy?"
3. "What would be a **prohibitively expensive** price — a non-starter?"

The magic is in the gap between answers. The "acceptable" number is a lowball. The "expensive" number is closer to true willingness to pay — people buy things they consider expensive all the time. The "prohibitive" number maps your ceiling. Ramanujam's related tactic: when a customer reacts to a price, ask *why*. "Too expensive" followed by silence is negotiation; "too expensive because our whole tooling budget is $X" is data. Do this with 15–20 prospects in one segment and you have a distribution, not an anecdote.

Also ask a follow-up almost nobody asks: **"How would you prefer to pay for this — per person, per [unit of work], flat?"** The answer tells you your pricing metric (section 4), which matters more than the number.

### 2b. Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter — with a worked example

The Van Westendorp PSM is a 1970s survey technique that is still the workhorse for early-stage pricing research, because it needs no existing sales data. You ask each respondent four questions about your product ([Wikipedia: Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp%27s_Price_Sensitivity_Meter)): **[Evergreen]**

1. At what price would this be **so expensive you wouldn't consider it**? (Too expensive)
2. At what price would it be **so cheap you'd doubt the quality**? (Too cheap)
3. At what price is it **getting expensive, but you'd still consider it**? (Expensive)
4. At what price is it a **bargain**? (Cheap/good value)

You then plot, for each question, the cumulative percentage of respondents at each price, and read off where the curves intersect. The **Point of Marginal Cheapness (PMC)** and **Point of Marginal Expensiveness (PME)** bound the acceptable range. The **Optimal Price Point (OPP)** — where the "too cheap" and "too expensive" curves cross — is where the fewest people reject the price outright.

**Worked example.** You survey 40 heads of engineering about a dollars-per-month price for an AI code-review tool. Median answers come back: too cheap $15, bargain $40, getting expensive $120, too expensive $300. When you plot all 40 responses, the curves cross at PMC ≈ $35, OPP ≈ $90, PME ≈ $150. Reading: below $35 you trigger quality doubt; the range $35–$150 is defensible; $90 minimizes outright rejection. Given the "charge more" bias you're about to read in section 3 — and the known limitation that Van Westendorp measures *acceptability*, not demand — a sane launch price is at or above the OPP, e.g. $99–$149, not the $40 "bargain" number your inner coward prefers. Run it per segment; a 5-person startup and a 500-person company will give you different curves, which is your first evidence you need tiers.

Caveats: Van Westendorp asks people to imagine prices, so it systematically compresses the top end — people understate what they'd actually pay under a real proposal with real return-on-investment math. Treat its output as a floor-finder and range-finder, not gospel. **[Evergreen]**

### 2c. Pricing A/B tests: mostly not for you (yet)

(An **A/B test**, also called a split test, randomly shows different versions of something — here, different prices — to different visitors and compares which performs better.)

The instinct "let's A/B test three price points on the landing page" runs into hard constraints at seed stage: **[Evergreen]**

- **Sample size.** Price tests need conversions (visitors who actually buy), not raw visitors, to produce a statistically trustworthy result. At a 2% conversion rate you need thousands of visitors per test group; a pre-launch startup has dozens.
- **Fairness and legal exposure.** Showing different customers different prices for the same thing at the same time can blow up publicly — Amazon got burned experimenting with this in 2000-era DVD pricing — and it complicates enterprise deals, where buyers talk to each other.
- **You'd learn the wrong thing.** Early on, your uncertainty isn't $99 vs. $119 — it's $99 vs. $999 vs. $9,999. That's a question for interviews and sales conversations, not a split test.

What works instead: **test in live sales conversations.** Quote a price with a straight face, watch the reaction, and raise it for the next prospect until people start pushing back hard. Deals closing instantly with zero negotiation = you're underpriced. This is the de facto standard seed-stage "A/B test." Save real statistical price testing for when you have PLG volume (product-led growth — see section 7). Even then, test *packaging* (what's in each tier) more than raw price points — packaging tests are less radioactive and usually higher-leverage. **[Evergreen]**

## 3. The classic mistake: underpricing. "Charge more."

If you take one tactical sentence from this document: **whatever you were planning to charge, it is probably too low.** This is the most repeated, most-validated piece of startup pricing advice in existence.

- **Marc Andreessen** (co-founder of a16z), asked by Tim Ferriss what he'd put on a billboard: "Right in the heart of San Francisco would be a billboard with just two words on it: **Raise Prices.**" His explanation: "the number one theme with our companies, when they get really struggling, is they are not charging enough for their product" — Silicon Valley's cheap-price-for-volume instinct kills companies that never generate enough gross profit to fund sales and growth ([Tim Ferriss Show #163 transcript](https://tim.blog/2018/01/01/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-marc-andreessen/)). **[Evergreen]**
- **Patrick McKenzie**, across two decades of essays: your customers can't build it themselves, they compare you to actual alternatives (agencies, headcount, doing nothing) rather than to your cost structure, and "your customers can only hold the fact that your product was built in a week against you if they know it was built in a week" ([You Can Probably Stand To Charge More](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2006/08/14/you-can-probably-stand-to-charge-more/)). In his consulting life he raised rates from $100/hour to $30,000/week and found demand *improved*: "Rates exert gravity. If you charge more, you'll spend your time talking to more sophisticated clients, working in better businesses, specializing in projects close to the money" ([Kalzumeus/Ramit Sethi podcast](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/09/21/ramit-sethi-and-patrick-mckenzie-on-why-your-customers-would-be-happier-if-you-charged-more/)). **[Evergreen]**

Why underpricing is structurally fatal, not just suboptimal:

1. **Price determines your go-to-market** (your plan for how you sell — self-serve on a website, or human salespeople, or both). At $30/month you can never afford a salesperson; at $30,000/year you can. If your product *needs* salespeople to win enterprise buyers, a low price forecloses that. (Y Combinator — the best-known startup accelerator, the program behind Airbnb, Stripe, and Dropbox — gives standard advice that runs the same direction: price is your best tool for learning, and it's much easier to lower a too-high price than to raise a too-low one.) **[Evergreen]**
2. **Price is a quality signal.** Van Westendorp's "too cheap" curve exists because buyers use price as a proxy for quality. Enterprise buyers distrust cheap tools with their critical workflows. **[Evergreen]**
3. **Underpricing doesn't even maximize adoption.** The Slack and HubSpot cases below show adoption comes from packaging (free tiers, low-friction entry), not from making the paid product cheap.
4. **You can't fund the business.** Gross profit per customer (revenue minus the direct cost of serving that customer) pays for support, sales, and product development. See `04-unit-economics.md`.

Practical rule: when you quote a price and nobody flinches, raise it 2× for the next prospect, and repeat until roughly 20–30% of qualified prospects push back or walk. Losing zero deals on price means you're too cheap. **[Evergreen]** heuristic.

## 4. The pricing metric: choosing the unit you charge by

Before the number, choose the **pricing metric** (Patrick Campbell calls it the "value metric") — the unit that scales your bill: per seat (per human user), per 1,000 contacts, per GB, per transaction, per resolved ticket, or a flat monthly fee. Campbell's core claim, from ProfitWell's research across thousands of SaaS companies: the value metric is the highest-leverage pricing decision, because "if you get your value metric right, if your customer sees the value in the same way you do, then there is no reason for your customers to churn" — **churn** meaning customers canceling ([Predictable Revenue interview with Patrick Campbell](https://medium.com/predictable-revenue/interview-with-profitwells-patrick-campbell-the-nuances-of-pricing-and-why-salespeople-aren-t-36d9e177f4aa); see also the [ProfitWell playbook summary at LTSE](https://ltse.com/insights/product-pricing-for-startups-value-metrics)). **[Evergreen]**

A good pricing metric passes three tests: **(1)** it tracks the value the customer receives — when they get more value, the metric goes up; **(2)** the customer can understand and predict it; **(3)** it grows as the customer grows, so your revenue expands without a re-sale. That third property is what produces net revenue retention above 100% (existing customers paying more over time; see `04-unit-economics.md`).

The main options: **[Evergreen]** framework, **[2026]** adoption numbers.

| Metric | Charges for | Best when | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Per seat** | Each human user | Value scales with people using it (collaboration tools, CRM — customer relationship management software, i.e. the system a sales team lives in, like Salesforce) | AI does the work → fewer humans → your revenue shrinks as your product improves |
| **Per usage** | Consumption (API calls — automated requests other software makes to your service — GB, compute, messages) | Value scales with volume; infrastructure products | Unpredictable bills scare buyers; revenue dips in customer slowdowns |
| **Per outcome** | Results delivered (resolved ticket, booked meeting, collected invoice) | Outcome is attributable and countable | Attribution fights; you underwrite performance risk |
| **Flat / tiered flat** | Access, in bands | Simplicity is a selling point; self-serve sales to small businesses | Big customers massively underpay; no expansion path |

Ramanujam's favorite illustration of metric innovation: **Michelin** stopped selling truck tires per tire and started charging fleets per mile driven — same product, ~20% more revenue, because the metric finally matched what customers actually valued ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/)). **[Evergreen]**

Industry drift, for calibration: OpenView (the VC firm whose SaaS benchmarks were the standard reference until it wound down in late 2023) tracked usage-based pricing adoption from 27% of SaaS companies (2018) to ~45% (2021), with a strong shift toward **hybrid** models — a subscription base plus usage components — rather than pure pay-as-you-go ([OpenView, The State of Usage-Based Pricing](https://openviewpartners.com/blog/state-of-usage-based-pricing/)). By 2026, hybrid is the fastest-growing model: in Kyle Poyar's May 2026 survey of 230 B2B software and AI companies (**B2B** = business-to-business, i.e. selling to companies rather than consumers), 37% run a hybrid model (e.g., a per-seat subscription with AI consumption on top), up from 25% just twelve months earlier. And when investors were asked which models they favor, hybrid (35%), outcome-based (26%), and usage-based (24%) crushed flat-fee (10%) and pure seat-based (5%) ([Growth Unhinged, The State of B2B Monetization in 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)). **[2026]**

## 5. Packaging and tiering: good-better-best, Goldilocks, anchors, decoys

**Packaging** = which features go in which bundle; **tiering** = the price ladder. Most companies converge on **good-better-best**: three public tiers. This is not blind imitation — it exploits real, robust buyer psychology: **[Evergreen]**

- **The Goldilocks effect (extremeness aversion):** given three options, buyers disproportionately choose the middle one. Your job: make the middle tier the one you actually want to sell, use the top tier to make it look reasonable, and use the bottom tier as an entry point and a catch for price-sensitive buyers.
- **Anchoring:** the first number a buyer sees becomes the reference point everything else is judged against. This is why pricing pages list the enterprise/top tier, and why sales decks open with the value number ("this saves you $2M/year") *before* the price ($150K suddenly sounds small). Order matters: show value, then price; show top tier, then middle. **[Evergreen]**
- **Decoy effect:** an intentionally unattractive option shifts choices toward the target. The canonical demo is Dan Ariely's *Economist* subscription experiment (*Predictably Irrational*, 2008), run on 100 MIT students: web-only $59, print-only $125, print+web $125. Print-only is a decoy nobody picks (0% chose it) — but its presence flipped the majority choice from the $59 option to the $125 bundle: **84%** chose the print+web bundle with the decoy present (16% web-only), versus only **32%** choosing the bundle once the decoy was removed (68% then took web-only). **[Evergreen]**

**Feature gating** — deciding which features live in which tier — should follow demand data, not engineering effort. Ramanujam's method: classify features as **leaders** (features people will pay for — anchor tiers around them), **fillers** (nice-to-haves — bundle them in), and **killers** (features a segment actively doesn't want — forcing people to pay for these kills deals; unbundle them). The rough survey to run: ask prospects to rank features by "would you pay for this?" and gate the top-ranked, low-overlap features into higher tiers. Classic gates that work: seat/volume caps, admin and security features (SSO — single sign-on, letting employees log in through their company's central identity system — is famously an enterprise-tier gate), integrations, audit/compliance, and support SLAs (service-level agreements — contractual promises about response times and uptime). **[Evergreen]**

**Worked example of a tier ladder** for the invoice-reconciliation product from section 1: Starter $500/mo (1 entity, 500 invoices/mo, email support) → Growth $1,500/mo (3 entities, 5,000 invoices, approval workflows, priority support) → Enterprise from $60K/yr (unlimited, SSO, audit log, ERP integrations — **ERP** = enterprise resource planning, the big central system a company runs its accounting and operations on, like SAP or NetSuite — plus dedicated support). The Enterprise anchor makes $1,500 look moderate. The invoice-count metric grows with the customer. SSO and audit gating mean any serious mid-market company (bigger than a small business, smaller than a giant enterprise) self-selects upward.

## 6. Enterprise pricing mechanics: list price, discounts, annual prepay

Once deals are sales-negotiated (roughly $10K+/year), pricing becomes a *system* with unwritten rules: **[Evergreen]**

- **List price vs. realized price.** Your published or quoted price is an opening position. Enterprise buyers *expect* to negotiate; procurement teams are paid to extract discounts. Standard practice: set list price high enough that a 20–30% discount lands where you actually want to be. A first "lighthouse" customer (a big recognizable name that validates you to other buyers) might get 50%+ — but trade it explicitly for something: a case study, a reference call, a multi-year commitment. Never discount for nothing; it trains buyers that your price is fiction.
- **Annual prepay.** The norm in B2B SaaS: annual contract, paid upfront, typically at a 15–20% discount versus monthly. Slack's was ~17%: $6.67/user/month billed annually versus $8 month-to-month ([Slack pricing case study](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/plg-monetization-case-study-lessons-from-slacks-bottom-up-pricing-strategy)). Why you want it: cash today funds growth without **dilution** (selling more shares of your company to raise money) — 12 months of cash upfront can matter more than the ~17% you gave up — and an annual commitment slashes churn. See `05-fundraising.md`; prepaid annual contracts are the cheapest financing you will ever get.
- **Never negotiate price alone.** Every concession should buy you term length, case-study rights, expansion commitments, or payment timing. "Discount ladder" discipline (e.g., 10% for annual, +10% for 2-year, +5% for a public reference) keeps your realized prices coherent.
- **Don't publish enterprise pricing.** "Contact sales" on the top tier is standard precisely because enterprise value varies 10× between customers, and you want room to price each deal on value. About one-third of AI apps keep pricing fully non-public ([Growth Unhinged, state of AI pricing](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/how-ai-apps-make-money)). **[2026]** stat, **[Evergreen]** logic.

## 7. PLG pricing

PLG (product-led growth: users adopt the product on their own, self-serve, before any salesperson appears — see `02-business-models.md`) inverts the pricing problem. The free tier is your marketing budget, and the paid line must sit exactly where habitual usage crosses into serious usage. Design rules: **[Evergreen]**

- **Gate on a usage dimension that grows with engagement,** not on core value. Slack's free tier gave you the *full* product but capped searchable history at 10,000 messages — a limit teams hit 1–3 months in, exactly when they were dependent ([Slack case study](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/plg-monetization-case-study-lessons-from-slacks-bottom-up-pricing-strategy)). The free product must be genuinely good, or it markets against you.
- **Price low-friction at entry, expand via the metric.** PLG paid tiers are credit-card priced ($8–$30/seat/month territory) so a team lead can expense them without procurement. Revenue then scales as seats and usage grow, and a sales team harvests the biggest accounts into enterprise contracts. 93% of Slack's $100K+ customers began as small self-serve deployments.
- **Make billing feel fair.** Slack's **Fair Billing Policy** — you're only charged for *active* users, with automatic prorated credits (refunds proportional to unused time) for inactive ones — attacked SaaS "shelfware" (paid-for seats nobody uses) and removed a major buying objection. It looked like leaving money on the table. It actually helped drive Slack to ~$400M ARR (annual recurring revenue — the yearly run-rate of subscription revenue, the standard yardstick for a SaaS company's size) in four years, largely without salespeople, with 143% net dollar retention in 2019 (**NDR** — revenue from last year's customers as a percentage of what those same customers paid last year; above 100% means the customers you keep pay you *more* each year, so the business grows even before adding anyone new), per Slack's disclosures around its IPO (initial public offering — when a company first sells shares to the public) ([Slack's Fair Billing Policy](https://slack.com/help/articles/218915077-Slacks-Fair-Billing-Policy); [case study](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/plg-monetization-case-study-lessons-from-slacks-bottom-up-pricing-strategy)). Trust-generating pricing is a growth weapon. **[Evergreen]**

Current-era benchmark: about half of leading AI apps have a free plan, and another fifth a free trial ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/how-ai-apps-make-money)). **[2026]**

## 8. Raising prices, and grandfathering

You will underprice v1 no matter what this doc says, so plan the correction. **[Evergreen]** mechanics:

- **Cadence:** revisit pricing every 6–12 months. Patrick Campbell's long-standing finding: companies that revisit pricing regularly monetize dramatically better than the set-and-forget majority. Early stage, treat every 5–10 new customers as a chance to test a higher quote.
- **New customers first.** The safest raise: new price for new customers, existing customers untouched. Zero churn risk, immediate signal on whether the market bears it.
- **Grandfathering** = letting existing customers keep their old price (fully, or for a defined period). Full permanent grandfathering is generous, but it creates a growing revenue drag and a support burden of legacy plans. The standard compromise: grandfather for 6–12 months, then migrate with notice, ideally sweetening with features ("your plan is going away; the new plan costs 30% more and includes X and Y"). Communicate early, explain the why (costs, new capability), and give a lock-in option ("renew annually now at your current price"). Handled well, price increases churn far fewer customers than founders fear — the customers you lose are disproportionately your least-engaged, worst-fit ones.
- **Raise with the value story, not apologetically.** Tie the increase to shipped value. HubSpot's 2020 repricing is the model: they moved to "marketing contacts" — you pay only for contacts you actually market to, storing the rest free — which let them *raise* effective prices for heavy users while making the change feel like a fairness improvement ([HubSpot, "Why HubSpot's Pricing Transformation Was Hard (But Necessary)"](https://medium.com/swlh/marketing-contacts-pricing-lessons-38eef8301e95)). **[Evergreen]**

## 9. The 2026 AI layer

Everything above still holds for AI products; these five things are new. All **[2026]** unless noted.

**1. You can price against labor budgets, not software budgets.** This is the single biggest change. When your AI product *does the work*, rather than helping a human do it, the budget you displace is headcount — and labor budgets are an order of magnitude bigger than software budgets. a16z: AI software "sells the work output itself, instead of selling software to help the people do the work," which means "AI companies are replacing labor budgets, whose size exceeds traditional software budgets, giving AI companies meaningfully larger contract sizes than their historical software counterparts" ([a16z, From Demos to Deals](https://a16z.com/insights-for-enterprise-ai-builders/)). (You may see specific dollars-per-employee AI-spend projections floating around; treat unattributed ones skeptically — the directional claim, labor budgets ≫ software budgets, is the load-bearing part.) Worked example: an AI SDR product (SDR = sales development rep — the person who books sales meetings) replacing one $70K/year human isn't competing with $99/month sales software; a $2,000–$3,000/month price is a 50–65% cost reduction against the *labor* comparable. The value-based framework from section 1 is unchanged — only the comparable is new. Principle **[Evergreen]**, numbers **[2026]**.

**2. Per-seat is breaking where AI removes seats.** If AI resolves the tickets, seat counts fall as your product gets better — your pricing metric now punishes your own success. a16z flags exactly this (Zendesk charging per support agent while AI shrinks agent counts) as the force pushing the industry toward usage and outcomes ([a16z enterprise newsletter, Dec 2024](https://a16z.com/newsletter/december-2024-enterprise-newsletter-ai-is-driving-a-shift-towards-outcome-based-pricing/)).

**3. Credits/token pricing is the messy middle.** (**Tokens** are the small chunks of text — roughly three-quarters of a word each — that AI models process and bill by.) Kyle Poyar (former OpenView operating partner, now the leading independent SaaS-pricing analyst at Growth Unhinged) documents the mass move to **credit systems** — customers buy a monthly pool of credits, and different AI actions burn different amounts — at Microsoft, Salesforce, OpenAI, Cursor, Adobe, HubSpot, Replit and others. Why: inference didn't get cheap the way everyone assumed (users always want the newest, priciest model — output-token prices for frontier models, the newest and most capable ones, barely fell from 2024 to 2025), consumption is wildly skewed ("70–80% of token consumption comes from just 10% of users"), and credits let vendors reprice actions without repricing the product ([Growth Unhinged, AI credit pricing](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/ai-credit-pricing)). The cost: buyers hate opacity — credits are "hard to predict, hard to manage, and feel like a black-box," and procurement fears runaway bills. If you use credits: publish a legible exchange rate, offer spend caps and alerts, and expect to bundle a predictable base allotment.

**4. Outcome-based pricing is powerful and dangerous.** Charging per *result* — Intercom's Fin at $0.99 per resolved support conversation is the canonical example ([Fin pricing](https://fin.ai/pricing/): "Resolutions... are $0.99 each"); EvenUp (legal claims) and Decagon (per-resolution option) are others ([a16z](https://a16z.com/newsletter/december-2024-enterprise-newsletter-ai-is-driving-a-shift-towards-outcome-based-pricing/)) — is the purest possible value alignment. The customer pays nothing for failure, which collapses buying risk and shortens sales cycles ("customers only pay when the product works" is the sales pitch it hands you). Poyar's 2026 survey data backs the momentum: investors named outcome-based (26%) and hybrid (35%) as their preferred models, far ahead of flat-fee or per-seat. But he also warns that many enterprises still crave predictability, and offers a **CAMP test** for whether outcome pricing can work for you: **C**onsistency of outcomes, **A**ttribution, **M**easurability, **P**redictability ([Growth Unhinged, The State of B2B Monetization in 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)). In plain terms: use it only when the outcome is unambiguous and countable, attribution to your product is clean, and you have enough performance data to underwrite the risk (i.e., absorb it yourself, the way an insurer does) — because a price tied to results invites the customer to scrutinize every billed outcome. Otherwise you've reinvented being a contingency-fee law firm — one paid only when it wins — with worse margins.

**5. Margin pressure is real — know your inference cost per unit.** **Gross margin** = the percentage of each revenue dollar left after paying the direct costs of serving the customer (servers, inference/API calls, support) — before salaries, marketing, and rent. Classic SaaS runs 75–85% gross margins; AI-native products often start at 50–65%, because every user action burns paid tokens (see `04-unit-economics.md` and `10-ai-era.md` for full treatment). Pricing implication: you must know your **cost per unit of value delivered** (e.g., "one resolved ticket costs us $0.11 of inference") and price with a floor above it. Flat-rate unlimited plans are how AI startups die: the 10% of power users who burn 70–80% of tokens will be underwater on any flat price that the other 90% accept. Hence the dominant 2026 pattern: **hybrid seat + usage** — a platform/seat fee that covers predictable value and preserves margin, plus usage/credits that scale with consumption (Cursor: seats plus usage for premium models). Poyar's data shows pure per-seat and pure pay-as-you-go both losing share to this hybrid ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/how-ai-apps-make-money)).

## 10. The pricing determination worksheet

Run this on any idea. Half a day of desk work plus 15–20 conversations. Steps 1–5 are **[Evergreen]** method; calibrate numbers against **[2026]** benchmarks above.

**Step 1 — Hypothesize the value metric.** Complete: "My customer gets more value from this when ___ increases." List 2–3 candidates (seats? invoices processed? tickets resolved? meetings booked?). Check each: tracks value received / predictable by the buyer / grows as the customer grows. Circle one.

**Step 2 — Quantify the value for one specific segment.** Pick ONE segment (e.g., "Shopify merchants doing $5–50M GMV" — gross merchandise value, the total dollar value of goods sold through a store or marketplace, before any fees or costs). Build the value equation with real numbers: hours saved × loaded hourly cost (the fully-loaded rate from section 1 — salary plus benefits, taxes, and overhead, ≈1.3× base salary, divided by ~2,000 working hours/year) + revenue gained + risk reduced. Write down the annual value number. Decide your comparable: software budget or labor budget? (If your product does the work, use labor — it's 10× bigger.)

**Step 3 — Set the hypothesis price at ~10–20% of value,** expressed in your value metric. Sanity-check against: (a) competitor/adjacent-tool anchors the buyer already knows, (b) your unit cost floor (inference + serving cost per metric-unit — require ≥70% gross margin at the hypothesis price, i.e., direct serving costs eat no more than 30 cents of each revenue dollar, or know exactly why not), (c) the go-to-market it implies (under ~$5K/yr must be self-serve; $25K+/yr can carry salespeople — see `02-business-models.md`).

**Step 4 — Test willingness to pay.** In 15–20 prospect conversations: pitch the problem/solution, then run Ramanujam's acceptable/expensive/prohibitive questions and ask their preferred pricing metric. Optionally field a Van Westendorp survey (at least 30 respondents from one segment) and compute PMC/OPP/PME. Then quote your hypothesis price in real sales conversations with a straight face. If fewer than 20% push back, raise it and re-test. Record every reaction verbatim.

**Step 5 — Package into three tiers.** Middle tier = the price and package you actually want to sell, aimed at your core segment. Top tier = anchor + enterprise gates (SSO, audit, SLA, "contact sales"). Bottom tier = entry point, gated on the usage metric so growth forces upgrades. If PLG: add a free tier capped on an engagement dimension (like Slack's 10K messages), not on core value. Set annual prepay at ~15–20% off monthly.

**Step 6 — Ship, measure, revisit in 6 months.** Watch: close rate (the share of sales conversations that end in a signed deal) by price quoted, discount depth, expansion revenue (is the metric growing inside accounts?), and gross margin per account (AI: token burn by cohort — a cohort being the group of customers who started in the same period). New price for new customers first; grandfather existing customers for 6–12 months with a value-framed migration.

## 11. Case studies: three pricing evolutions

**Slack (PLG, per-active-seat).** Free tier with the full product but a 10K-message history cap → conversion triggered exactly when dependence formed. Fair Billing (pay only for active users) removed the shelfware objection. 20% annual-prepay discount. Result: ~$400M ARR in four years, 143% NDR in 2019, 93% of $100K+ accounts starting bottom-up — small teams adopting on their own, then spreading through the company ([case study](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/plg-monetization-case-study-lessons-from-slacks-bottom-up-pricing-strategy)). Lesson: generosity in *billing fairness* plus discipline in *where the free tier ends* beats a low price. **[Evergreen]**

**HubSpot (metric migration).** 2006: tiers based on features + total contact-database size. As databases filled with dead emails, "pay per stored contact" drifted away from value delivered → customer resentment. 2020: repriced to **marketing contacts** — pay only for contacts you actively market to; store up to a million others free ([HubSpot on Medium](https://medium.com/swlh/marketing-contacts-pricing-lessons-38eef8301e95)); later added seat-based components across its Free/Starter/Professional/Enterprise hub tiers ([pricing evolution overview](https://newsletter.pricingsaas.com/p/hubspots-pricing-evolution)). Lesson: when your metric stops mirroring value, migrate the metric — even though it's operationally brutal — and frame the raise as fairness. **[Evergreen]** lesson.

**Intercom Fin (AI-native, outcome-based).** Intercom historically charged per support seat. Its AI agent Fin charges $0.99 **per resolved conversation** — a "resolution" meaning the customer asks for no further help after Fin's last answer; no resolution, no charge ([Fin pricing page](https://fin.ai/pricing/)). This prices directly against the labor cost of a human resolution (dollars, not cents), makes buying nearly risk-free, and sidesteps the seat-shrinkage trap of section 9.2 — while transferring performance risk to the vendor, which Intercom can carry because it has millions of conversations of data to underwrite it. Lesson: outcome pricing is earned with data, and the *unit* ("resolution") was chosen because it's countable and attributable. **[2026]**

---

The meta-lesson across all three: none of these companies found the right price on day one. They found the right **metric**, watched where it diverged from value, and repriced deliberately. Price is a product — version it.
